### Salvation is a truly free gift, brought about solely by the blood of Christ

{{% summary %}}

Salvation is God's free gift to humanity, purchased by the blood of Christ alone. Christ's spiritual death on the cross, not His physical blood, is what satisfied both God's justice and God's love and opened the way for our reconciliation to God. Humans cannot earn salvation. We are not worthy to pay for a single one of our sins. To be saved, all that God requires is our simple faith in Christ. Relying on anything other than faith in Jesus is saying that the cross was not enough, and turns Christianity a religion of works, rather than what it truly is---undeserved grace offered by a loving God.

What all of this means is that salvation 1) comes through simple faith, 2) not as a result of anything we do, and 3) was paid for solely by the blood of Christ.

To say that anything else is necessary for salvation is to say that the blood of Christ alone is not enough. And that, to my mind, is completely blasphemous. As I see things, we should in fact be that black and white about it, and not be particularly polite about this point. That is, if anyone insists that anything else is necessary for salvation, we should be outspoken and bold in proclaiming that taking such a position is logically equivalent to spitting upon the cross.

Despite being just about as core a teaching as any in Christianity, God's truly free gift of salvation comes under near-constant attack, even in the modern Church. This is because people try to add to what we have gone over here in one way or another. All such additions are problematic, and turn Christianity into a religion of works, not one of undeserved grace.

How do we know if we are saved? Does sin affect our salvation? Here we discuss assurance of salvation. Assurance should not be based upon our own human efforts, for human beings are incapable of saving ourselves, and it must not be based on feeling, because human emotions do not decide the truth. ### Is water baptism necessary for salvation?

{{< properties

{{% summary %}}

Here we examine some of the common Bible passages that people cite to say that water baptism is *necessary* for salvation. When these passages are properly translated and explained, it is clear that the Bible does *not* support this position. The blood of Christ is what purchased our salvation, and faith in Him alone is what saves us. Water baptism is a physical work, and has no power to save.

Other people hold that water baptism is not necessary for salvation, but still something that God desires us to do. Water baptism was John's baptism, commanded for the Early Church, which was comprised mostly of Jewish people. In our dispensation of the Church, water baptism is not authorized. When a person believes in Christ, they are immediately baptized into the body of Christ through the Holy Spirit. Salvation is by faith in Christ *alone*, meaning that absolutely nothing else can be inserted into the process without contradicting that statement.

{{% /summary %}}

{{< /quizdown >}}

<!-- slide-break -->

Those of us in the [Ichthys](https://ichthys.com/) orbit do not actually hold that water baptism is even something generally authorized in the Church Age (apart from the transitional period in the early church when believers were overwhelming Jewish, and therefore conversant with John's baptism). We won't fully get into it here since dealing with all the implications of spirit baptism vs. water baptism is a study all on its own (if you are curious, I might suggest you start with [Jordan Bomberger's take on some of the relevant passages, on BibleDriven.org](https://www.bibledriven.org/large-topical/a-special-treatment-on-water-baptism/)), however, we will briefly address certain passages sometimes brought in by people that do formally argue that water baptism is *necessary* for salvation.

I should actually further clarify that many Christians who do prescribe water baptism do not make it a prerequisite for salvation. The common way of phrasing things is that faith alone saves us, but water baptism is "an outward sign of an inward change that God nonetheless wants us to do" or something along those lines. This is probably the most common position held by Evangelicals, and even Protestants more generally. I still disagree with even this, but at least it doesn't butcher soteriology quite so bad.

<!-- slide-break -->

At any rate, some of those proof-text passages that are trotted out by some to argue that water baptism is strictly necessary for salvation:

{{% scripture p="Mark 16:16 | NIV11" %}}

**16** Whoever believes and is baptized will be saved, but whoever does not believe will be condemned.

{{% /scripture %}}

<!-- slide-break -->

{{% scripture p="1 Peter 3:18-22 | NIV11" %}}

**18** For Christ also suffered once for sins, the righteous for the unrighteous, to bring you to God. He was put to death in the body but made alive in the Spirit. **19** After being made alive, he went and made proclamation to the imprisoned spirits— **20** to those who were disobedient long ago when God waited patiently in the days of Noah while the ark was being built. In it only a few people, eight in all, were saved through water, **21** and this water symbolizes baptism that now saves you also—not the removal of dirt from the body but the pledge of a clear conscience toward God. It saves you by the resurrection of Jesus Christ, **22** who has gone into heaven and is at God’s right hand—with angels, authorities and powers in submission to him.

{{% /scripture %}}

<!-- slide-break -->

{{% scripture p="John 3:5 | NIV11" %}}

**5** Jesus answered, “Very truly I tell you, no one can enter the kingdom of God unless they are born of water and the Spirit.

{{% /scripture %}}

<!-- slide-break -->

Mark 16:16 is probably the easiest passage to handle. This is part of the so-called ["long ending of Mark"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mark_16#Longer_ending_2), and is not even part of scripture, because it is a textual interpolation. So that is that.

As to 1 Peter 3:18-22, this passage is quite difficult to interpret, partially because often translators fundamentally misunderstand Peter's point, and therefore translate in such a way that it more completely obfuscates the meaning in English. It's not that this passage about "baptism" in 1 Peter 3 is particularly easy to interpret even in the Greek, but the point is that most English translations *really* make it hard to interpret it correctly, and this leads to most people thinking that this passage is talking about water baptism, when really, it isn't. For a detailed explanation of this, see [here](https://ichthys.com/Pet35.htm#18-22). Dr. Luginbill's translation of verse 21:

{{% ichthys-translation p="1st Peter 3:21" %}}

And analogously it is [this true] baptism [of the Spirit] which now saves you: [you are saved] ***not*** through any [literal] washing away of filth from your flesh, but [through] an appeal to God for a clean conscience through the resurrection of Jesus Christ.

{{% /ichthys-translation %}}

<!-- slide-break -->

Finally, as to John 3:5, some people take the "water" here to be a reference to baptism. It is not; it is a reference to the truth of the Word of God. See:

- [Email: John's Water-Baptism versus the Baptism of the Holy Spirit, §Q/A #1](https://ichthys.com/mail-Johns-Baptism-versus-Spirit-Baptism.htm)
- [BB6A: Peripateology: The Study of the Christian Walk, §The Water of the Word](https://ichthys.com/6A-Peripateo.htm#1._The_Water_of_the_Word)

<!-- slide-break -->

We might go on and knock down additional passages, but hopefully this brief sampling is sufficient to show that the position that water baptism is necessary for salvation is clearly false, and not supported by the Bible.

Moreover, there is also the wider point that if salvation is by faith *alone*, then even if you wanted to somehow insert water baptism into soteriology, logic forbids it. (We will continue to make this general argument as we knock down other false works-based requirements for salvation too). I have had some Eastern Orthodox friends tell me with a straight face that in their opinion, getting water baptized *is* faith (not works), and that is how they have somehow convinced themselves that the propositions "you must be water baptized to be saved" and "we are saved by faith alone" do not contradict each other. Personally, I find this unconvincing in the extreme.

<!-- slide-break -->

Finally, as a parting *reductio ad arbsurdum* argument, every single person who argues that you must be water baptized in order to be saved necessarily teaches that it is possible for someone to believe in their heart that Jesus is Lord and savior, but get hit by a bus on their way to get water baptized, and subsequently end up in hell.

If they try to say that this hypothetical person would actually be saved because they have the *intention* to be water baptized, it still logically proves that water baptism itself is not necessary for salvation, QED.

{{% summary %}}

Matthew 28:19, the Great Commission passage, is often used as a proof text to support water baptism. However, English versions often mistranslate the Greek text. In the phrase, "baptizing them in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit", the word "*in*" is actually "*into*" in the Greek. The Holy Spirit baptizes us *into* Christ, and we become spiritually united with God. In other words, this verse is referring to Spirit baptism, rather than water baptism.  

For a short time in the early Church, the Holy Spirit was physically transferred by the laying on of hands, at times mediated through water baptism.

However, this physical transferring stopped not long after the Church age began, because by the time Romans was written, Paul says that anyone without the Holy Spirit is not a believer (Romans 8:9). To make Paul's statement there logically compute, the Holy Spirit must now indwell a person immediately upon their faith in Christ, and not a second later.

### Is belonging to some specific group necessary for salvation?

{{< properties

ptags="Matthew 7:21-23; 1 John 2:4"

stags=""

timestamp=""

content-end-card-next-video=""

content-end-card-suggestion=""

content-short-title="Is belonging to some specific group necessary for salvation?"

content-thumbnail-description="Black background with white text. Has text 'Specific works' then an equals sign, followed by 'salvation?'"

>}}

{{% summary %}}

Some people claim that belonging to their specific group is necessary for salvation. This is typical of cults proper, and is an effective manipulation tactic. Rather than claiming this outright, however, they might make an argument from scripture that "faith without works is dead" and then twist this truth and attempt to say that people who are *truly* saved will follow *their* teachings. The Bible does clearly state that faith without works is dead (and thus, that all who truly believe will bear some fruit of faith), but this is very different than claiming that "all who are truly saved will bear this specific fruit that our group prioritizes."

Christians remain imperfect even after belief---doing things we ought not, and failing to do things we ought---but nevertheless, all genuine believers bear *some* fruit. However, not all believers will necessarily bear the *same* fruit. Yet that is precisely what cults claim: that if someone does not exhibit the specific fruit they require, then that person is not actually saved, no matter what they may claim to believe about Christ with their lips. In this way, many groups that focus on explicit membership distinctions center around the group defining what works of faith are "required" for salvation, picking and choosing. The problem with this is that Christians are actually called to *complete perfection* (Matthew 5:48). There is therefore no distinction between "mandatory things" and "optional things" for believers. And if that is true, then how can any list of "required" fruit be anything other than someone's mere opinion? Short answer: it necessarily cannot be anything other than exactly that. *Mere opinion*.

As should be obvious, salvation has nothing to these arbitrary lists of behaviors that are mere opinion. Instead, as we have been saying all along, salvation is only ever received through simple faith in Christ, and that alone.

{{% /summary %}}

{{< quizdown >}}

{{< /quizdown >}}

<!-- slide-break -->

Note the word "specific" in the title on this section. While I suppose some people may believe that you must be a formal member of good standing in some physical brick-and-mortar church in order to actually be saved, I think most of the time the people who are pushy about such a thing don't treat it as a matter of salvation, but a matter of "what God wants us to do" (compare what we talked about before with the conventional Evangelical view of water baptism as being something God wants us to do, even if it is not directly tied to salvation). So instead of this section being about consistently showing up somewhere physical on Sunday mornings (or something to that effect), what we have in our cross-hairs here is more people who treat membership in their particular group as being a prerequisite for salvation.

This teaching is very characteristic of cults proper. Most cults teach some form of "only group members are saved", and then use this as a club to bully and manipulate people. After all, when it is salvation on the line, people will be willing to do a lot. Nobody wants to go to hell, or miss out on heaven.

<!-- slide-break -->

Of course, many cults are smarter in their PR than being quite so blatant about it, and instead try to dress it up as "all people who are truly saved act they way we act, and everyone else is just deluding themselves that they are saved". The scary thing is that the clever ones will make an argument from scripture. For example:

{{% scripture p="Matthew 7:21-23 | ESV" %}}

**21** “Not everyone who says to me, ‘Lord, Lord,’ will enter the kingdom of heaven, but the one who does the will of my Father who is in heaven. **22** On that day many will say to me, ‘Lord, Lord, did we not prophesy in your name, and cast out demons in your name, and do many mighty works in your name?’ **23** And then will I declare to them, ‘I never knew you; depart from me, you workers of lawlessness.’

{{% /scripture %}}

{{% scripture p="1 John 2:4 | NIV11" %}}

**4** Whoever says, “I know him,” but does not do what he commands is a liar, and the truth is not in that person.

{{% /scripture %}}

<!-- slide-break -->

They will say that their commands are equal to the commands of God mentioned in 1 John 2:4; that not doing what they say means you will find yourself turned away on judgement day, knocking but refused entry because you supposedly "failed to do what God wanted of you". (Never mind the fact that the things they say you have to do come from them not God).

This is a twisted form of what is typically termed ["lordship salvation"](https://www.gotquestions.org/lordship-salvation.html). The general idea that faith without works is dead is clearly taught in scripture, and therefore that true belief always more than "mere intellectual assent" (to echo the terminology of those who typically criticize so-called ["easy-believism"](https://www.gotquestions.org/easy-believism.html)).

The particular means of manipulation that cults use here is quite clever, because what they argue for really isn't salvation by works proper. What do I mean? Well, they may affirm this proposition:

> It is *not* bearing fruit itself that saves, but instead it is true faith (that will thereafter necessarily cause fruit to be borne in the life of the believer) that saves.

But just say that the particular behaviors they specify are the only "true fruit", and therefore that all people who are actually saved will exhibit these behaviors. They will be quick to say that it is not that these behaviors themselves save, but that all who are saved will exhibit these behaviors.

<!-- slide-break -->

Upon first examination, this might seem to tie our hands. We might know intuitively that the group is adding burdensome legalistic practices on top of belief, but when they hide behind the above reasoning, what can we actually do to argue against it?

The critical thing to understand is that the true position that "faith without works is dead" and "all those with true faith bear fruit" is markedly different from "all people who are saved do XYZ specific actions". It is implicitly conflating these two things that gives cults and other groups manipulative power.

Clearly, we Christians remain imperfect even after belief (cf. Romans 3:23; James 3:2). This means that we will still do things we ought not, and fail to do things we ought. What I mean by this is that we may truly believe, and yet still not bear all the fruit we should. If our faith is genuine, we will bear *some* fruit, to be sure (and that is what the Bible clearly teaches), but that does not mean that everyone with saving faith will necessarily bear the *same* fruit of faith. The two propositions are logically distinct from each other.

The position that cults take is the latter: that everyone who is saved must bear XYZ specific fruit, and that if they don't then it *ipso facto* proves that their faith is not genuine faith. To use the imagery of the metaphor, they are essentially saying that all truly saved people will bear apples and pears (say), so if someone else bears grapes and strawberries, well, too bad so sad. Because their fruit isn't apples and pears, it means they aren't saved.

<!-- slide-break -->

When phrased in this way, I hope you can see how this is a bit ridiculous. Who are they to draw up an arbitrary list of fruits that are strictly necessary, while others somehow remain optional? In principle, we Christians are actually called to complete perfection (Matthew 5:48), with no distinction between "mandatory things" and "optional things". This means that any list-making is in the end nothing more than some human's idiosyncratic opinion about what is and is not important. And clearly such a thing can have nothing to do with salvation, QED.

Attacking this position lets us ignore appeals to group membership almost entirely, because at the end of the day, they all mostly boil down to this principle of the group arbitrarily defining what is and is not "required". It may not be direct salvation by works they argue for, it is true, but that doesn't stop it from still being utter nonsense.

### Is having good theology generally necessary for salvation?

{{< properties

ptags=""

{{% summary %}}

Some groups teach that one must either accept certain doctrines (such as Young Earth Creationism or Pentecostal "Spirit Baptism") in order to be saved, or that one must reject specific doctrines or repent of specific sins (such as homosexuality or transgenderism) in order to receive salvation. These doctrines are used to gatekeep "true believers". While these groups may not outright claim that anyone who doesn't agree with them is unsaved (although sometimes they may in fact go that far), their scorning of others as terrible Christians amounts to nearly the same.

The Bible teaches that the only requirement for salvation is true belief in Jesus Christ, which logically mandates that *nothing else* can be involved. While there is no excuse for sin, going so far as to take a firm stance that not-yet-repented-for sin precludes salvation would logically mean that *none of us at all could be saved*. Why? Because all of us have sin in our lives that we haven't repented for, and may not even yet recognize as sinful! Nothing in the Bible makes it impossible for someone to be enmeshed in serious sin and still be a believer (although such a position *is* extremely dangerous spiritually). Just look at King Saul.

However, this does *not* mean that we aren't allowed to properly deal with serious sin in the Church, per 1 Corinthians 5. We are not excusing sin or even saying that Christians are not allowed to cast out of our churches those who publicly and shamelessly flout everything good and true and godly. Rather, we are just taking the Bible at its word when it says that faith in Christ (and that alone) is what saves us.

#### Introduction: Doctrinal additions to belief in Jesus Christ

Some groups seem to teach *de facto* (if not *de jure*) that people must either 1) believe certain "important" teachings in order to be saved, or 2) reject certain problematic teachings in order to be saved.

I will certainly not pretend to be exhaustive here, but we will go over some of the common culprits that end up being viewed in this way.

<!-- slide-break -->

#### Things people must believe in order to be saved, supposedly

Belief in these things sometimes end up being used as a [Shibboleth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shibboleth) of sorts to gatekeep "true believers". While groups may not go so far as to outright claim that anyone that doesn't agree with these is necessarily damned, they sure act like they are terrible Christians and worthy of scorn, which might amount to very nearly the same.

<!-- slide-break -->

##### Calvinism (for or against)

While most people who agree with or disagree with Calvinism teach that people who subscribe to the other side are still saved so long as the believe in Jesus Christ, there are some extreme groups on either side that explicitly reject this.

Certain hyper-Calvinist groups may claim that only those who explicitly subscribe to five-point TULIP Calvinism—or somehow show specific signs of being part of the "elect" in a very rigid way—are truly regenerate or saved.

And on the other side, there are also those who argue that Calvinism is problematic enough that it makes salvation impossible:

- Such people might argue that the "God of Calvinism" is fundamentally different from the "God of the Bible". They might say that because Calvinism teaches Unconditional Election (i.e., that God chooses who goes to heaven and hell before they are even born, with their free will having nothing to do with it), it paints God as an outright monster, or as the author of sin. They might then argue that to worship such a God---one who does not genuinely love or desire the salvation of every individual---is to worship a false god or an idol, which prevents true salvation.
- Such people might also argue that five-point TULIP Calvinism qualifies as a proper soteriological heresy (i.e., a false doctrine concerning how humans are saved). They then may say that under Paul's warning in Galatians 1:8 ("If anyone preaches any other gospel to you... let him be accursed"), adherents of Calvinism are actually preaching a "different gospel" that cannot save.

Either way, the Bible just says that all that is necessary for salvation is belief in Jesus Christ. In my opinion, that obviously disproves both sides of this argument. People that want to be saved deep down in their heart aren't going to be sent to hell on a technicality. Whether they think God himself caused their belief due to His Irresistible Grace or they chose it themselves, either way they believe, right?

<!-- slide-break -->

##### Young Earth Creationism (YEC)

This one always makes me shake my head. Put simply, no matter how wrong someone is about the way the universe came to be, it has very little to do with salvation by grace through faith, right? At least this seems patently self-evident to me.

For what it is worth, some of the most prominent YEC organizations explicitly affirm that believing in a young earth is *not* required for salvation, so that is at least something:

- Ken Ham's [Answers in Genesis](https://answersingenesis.org/about/)
- The [Institute for Creation Research (ICR)](https://www.icr.org/)
- [Creation Ministries International (CMI)](https://creation.com/en-US)

Yet there are some hard-line young earth creationists out there who really do teach that it is a salvation issue. It is probably most common for it to be a bit indirect (so something like "believing in a young earth won't save you, but denying it will cause you to lose your salvation and/or prove you were never saved to begin with"), but still.

<!-- slide-break -->

##### "Spirit Baptism"/speaking in tongues

Most mainstream Pentecostals and Charismatics do not view so-called "Spirit Baptism" as being strictly required for salvation. Groups like the [Assemblies of God (AG)](https://ag.org/) and the [Church of God](https://churchofgod.org/)—as well as the vast majority of Charismatics—view salvation and the doctrine of secondary Spirit Baptism as two separate events:

- **Salvation**: They teach that a person is fully saved by grace through faith alone the moment they repent of their sins and accept Jesus Christ as Savior. They believe the Holy Spirit dwells within every genuine believer at conversion. They would emphatically state that a non-Pentecostal (such as a Baptist, Catholic, or Presbyterian) is 100% saved without ever experiencing the Pentecostal secondary Spirit Baptism.
- **Secondary Spirit Baptism**: They view Spirit Baptism as a secondary gift available to all believers *after* salvation. Its primary purpose is not to save, but to empower the believer with supernatural strength for witnessing and holy living.

However, there are some groups who believe that receiving secondary Spirit Baptism—specifically marked by the physical evidence of speaking in tongues—is considered an absolute requirement for salvation. Because they believe the book of Acts establishes that everyone who received the Holy Spirit spoke in tongues, they maintain that if you have never spoken in tongues, you have not truly received the Spirit. Citing Romans 8:9 ("If anyone does not have the Spirit of Christ, they do not belong to Christ"), they conclude that without Spirit Baptism and tongues, a person is not yet actually saved.

#### Things people must reject in order to be saved, supposedly

Rejection of these things sometimes end up being used as a [Shibboleth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shibboleth) of sorts to gatekeep "true believers". While groups may not go so far as to outright claim that anyone that fails to reject these is necessarily damned, they sure act like they are terrible Christians and worthy of scorn, which might amount to very nearly the same.

<!-- slide-break -->

##### Women pastors

While mainstream complementarian groups—such as the [Southern Baptist Convention (SBC)](https://www.sbc.net/) or prominent Reformed groups—strongly oppose women serving as pastors on biblical grounds, they generally frame it as an issue of ecclesiology and obedience, rather than a salvation issue.

However, there are certain fringe groups that do explicitly teach that affirming women pastors indicates a lack of true faith. The argument typically goes that because the prohibition against women teaching or exercising authority over men (such as in 1 Timothy 2:12) is so explicit, anyone who willfully ignores it is living in unrepentant, open rebellion against God.

<!-- slide-break -->

##### Homosexuality and transgenderism

Unlike the linking of rejecting women pastors with salvation (which is really only present in fringe groups), there are *lots* of mainstream evangelical organizations that explicitly teach that someone who is a practicing homosexual or trans person (or even someone who approves of it, even if they are not themselves homosexual/trans) simply cannot be saved.

The most common argument rests on the biblical definition of a Christian as a "regenerated" person who actively repents of sin. Adherents of this position will argue for their position using certain passages like 1 Corinthians 6:9-10:

{{% scripture p="1 Corinthians 6:9–10 | NIV11" %}}

**9** Or do you not know that wrongdoers will not inherit the kingdom of God? Do not be deceived: Neither the sexually immoral nor idolaters nor adulterers nor men who have sex with men **10** nor thieves nor the greedy nor drunkards nor slanderers nor swindlers will inherit the kingdom of God.

{{% /scripture %}}

They argue that while a Christian can certainly stumble into sin, a *true* believer cannot live in active, unrepentant, and celebrated sin. In this view, if a person identifies as homosexual or as trans (i.e., openly lives these lifestyles without a desire to repent), it serves as outward proof that their heart was never actually transformed by the Holy Spirit. They conclude that the individual possesses an intellectual belief rather than true, saving faith.

<!-- slide-break -->

Some go even further and claim that straight Christians, pastors, and churches who theologically affirm homosexuality also cannot be saved. This position makes an argument based on Romans 1:32, which warns against those who not only practice things worthy of death but "also approve of those who practice them."

For example, consider the [Nashville Statement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nashville_Statement) (a 2017  evangelical coalition document signed by hundreds of conservative leaders, including Southern Baptists and Presbyterian PCA theologians). One part of the statement says the following:

{{% quote work="Nashville Statement" workurl="https://cbmw.org/the-nashville-statement/" author="The Council on Biblical Manhood & Womanhood" authorurl="https://cbmw.org/" %}}

"We affirm that it is sinful to approve of homosexual immorality or transgenderism and that such approval constitutes an essential departure from Christian faithfulness and witness."

{{% /quote %}}

By calling it an "essential departure from Christian faithfulness," some people argue that teaching that homosexuality is acceptable is not a minor denominational disagreement. Instead, they teach that pastors or churchgoers who rewrite biblical sexual ethics are preaching a "false gospel" of cheap grace. Because a false gospel cannot save, those who trust in it are viewed as spiritually deceived and lacking saving faith.

<!-- slide-break -->

##### Abortion

The majority of traditional, conservative Christian denominations explicitly reject the idea that having an abortion permanently revokes a person's ability to be saved. It is viewed as a serious sin, but one that can nonetheless be confessed and washed clean (in the manner of 1 John 1:9), just like all other sins.

However, there are groups that view a persistent pro-choice stance as evidence of a "false conversion" or a lack of genuine saving faith. They teach that if someone actively supports ("believes in") abortion rights, they are willingly advocating for what these groups define as murder.

In this view, a person who remains pro-choice is living in a state of willful, unrepentant rebellion against God. These teachers argue that salvation requires actual repentance from sin, so a person cannot truly be saved while simultaneously holding a belief that affirms abortion.

<!-- slide-break -->

#### If we say that salvation is based solely on belief in Jesus Christ, then we have to accept *all* the logical consequences of that position

Put simply, the Bible only talks about belief in Jesus Christ. Not:

- Belief in Jesus Christ AND belief in secondary Spirit Baptism/speaking in tongues
- Belief in Jesus Christ AND belief that homosexuality/transgenderism is sinful
- Etc.

If we say that salvation is based solely on belief in Jesus Christ ("faith alone"), then that logically mandates that *nothing else* can be involved whatsoever. Full stop. That is simply all there is to it.

This means that having good theology generally is *not* necessary for salvation. People can believe in all sorts of wrong things, so long as they *also* believe in Jesus Christ as their personal Lord and Savior.

<!-- slide-break -->

#### But what about the sin?!

Particularly when it comes to homosexuality and transgenderism and abortion and other areas of serious sin, many conservative Christians will literally throw you out if you suggest that their arguments about "unrepentant rebellion in specific area XYZ necessarily means no saving faith" are theologically false.

In current 21st century America---with the culture war alive and well---even suggesting that maybe these things are not directly related to salvation is seen as compromising with the enemy, and going soft on sin. After all, we don't want to encourage these things, right?

Let's have a look at some of the main proof-text passages:

<!-- slide-break -->

{{% scripture p="1 Corinthians 6:9–10 | NIV11" %}}

**9** Or do you not know that wrongdoers will not inherit the kingdom of God? Do not be deceived: Neither the sexually immoral nor idolaters nor adulterers nor men who have sex with men **10** nor thieves nor the greedy nor drunkards nor slanderers nor swindlers will inherit the kingdom of God.

{{% /scripture %}}

<!-- slide-break -->

{{% scripture p="Galatians 5:19-21 | NIV11" %}}

**19** The acts of the flesh are obvious: sexual immorality, impurity and debauchery; **20** idolatry and witchcraft; hatred, discord, jealousy, fits of rage, selfish ambition, dissensions, factions **21** and envy; drunkenness, orgies, and the like. I warn you, as I did before, that those who live like this will not inherit the kingdom of God.

{{% /scripture %}}

<!-- slide-break -->

{{% scripture p="Ephesians 5:5 | NIV11" %}}

For of this you can be sure: No immoral, impure or greedy person—such a person is an idolater—has any inheritance in the kingdom of Christ and of God.

{{% /scripture %}}

<!-- slide-break -->

{{% scripture p="Revelation 22:15 | ESV" %}}

Outside are the dogs, the sorcerers, the sexually immoral, the murderers, the idolaters, and everyone who loves and practices falsehood.

{{% /scripture %}}

<!-- slide-break -->

One important thing to note upfront is that these passages are clearly not supposed to be exhaustive in their accounting of different types of sin. That is, just because some other particular sin doesn't make the lists here doesn't mean the thrust of these passages doesn't apply equally to it too.

Now, virtually everyone agrees that these verses do not mean that any person who sins in one of these areas just once is going be eternally damned thereafter. Rather, the disagreement comes from some people insisting that an unwillingness to *repent* for sin indicates a rebellious heart that has not truly experienced salvific regeneration through faith.

The main issue with this position is that *all of us* walk around with sin in our lives that we may not have yet repented for. Sometimes we don't even recognize it as sinful! (Not that that is any sort of legitimate excuse). So this being case, is it not true that taking a firm stand on this means that *none of us at all can be saved*?

Ah, but it is only *those* sorts of sins over there that operate in this way, not *our* sins. Says who? Based on what biblical argument?

We are right back to arbitrary lists of mandatory fruits, just like we explained before in regards to cults that impose idiosyncratic standards of behavior for group membership. *It's literally the same thing*.

<!-- slide-break -->

Now, this does not mean we are not allowed to put our foot down when it comes to serious sin in the Church. For example, unrepentant homosexuals who claim to be Christian should *not* be extended Christian fellowship, per 1 Corinthians 5; the Church ought to police itself from within. And in fact, we are also well within our rights to be *skeptical* of the genuineness of their faith. Clearly, people who do the spiritual equivalent of making black white are at the very least spiritual infants, and only one very small step from apostasy. However, this does not mean we get to assert that they *cannot* be saved. Being skeptical is a very different beast from complete epistemological certainty.

I understand that it can feel uncomfortable and wrong to say that those enmeshed in serious sin can be believers too, but nothing in the Bible makes this impossible. God's grace *is* completely scandalous, after all. Being willing to allow for the possibility does *not* mean we are at all excusing the sin, but just that we take the Bible at its word when it says that believing in Jesus Christ (and that alone) is what saves us.

Sin is not the issue when it comes to salvation, because Jesus paid for all human sin on the cross. While living in gross sin is a spiritually dangerous position, it does not *necessarily* mean that someone is unsaved... although I should be quick to note that although we cannot be dogmatic about serious sin proving that faith is not genuine, we are certainly allowed to question and be skeptical of the genuineness of the faith of those who make a mockery of the cross by their actions of gross disobedience.

Refusing to logically equate serious sin with unbelief does *not* mean we are encouraging or excusing sin. In fact, we are commanded *not* to extend Christian fellowship to someone claiming Christ but yet refusing to repent of their blatantly sinful behavior (1 Corinthians 5). But as we will continue to say on repeat, the Bible's position is that faith in Christ (and only that) is what is required for salvation. We do not get to twist that truth just because we are personally sickened and disgusted by the actions of other people claiming Christ with their lips, no matter how tempted we may be to do exactly that.
