#### Summary points {#summary-points}

This lesson is the first of two parts going over the Crusades, the Christian pilgrimage-holy-wars to Jerusalem and surrounding regions of the Middle East.

Few periods in history have the same level of popular investment as the Crusades, nor the same level of narrative-shaping impact (in terms of affecting how people think about broad trends or patterns in history and sociology). This has been ever more the case post-[9/11](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/September_11_attacks).

<!-- slide-break -->

In our modern times characterized by a renewed focus on religiously-motivated violence, the Crusades are something that is discussed frequently---not only at the level of everyday people, but also at the level of politics:

- For example, in 2001, not long after 9/11, speaking at [Georgetown](https://www.georgetown.edu/), [Bill Clinton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bill_Clinton) placed the blame for faith-based violence on religious extremism---and not just that within [Islam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islam). He said the religious extremism of the Middle Ages had caused the Crusades and all the atrocities therein. (Note the strong language of moral condemnation; "atrocities" is far from a neutral label).
- [George W. Bush](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_W._Bush) also used language and imagery related to what happened in the Crusades when speaking about the [Iraq War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iraq_War)... or at least the ongoing popular understanding of what happened in the Crusades.
- More recently, [Barack Obama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barack_Obama), speaking at a prayer breakfast, urged those who identify as Christians to remain humble because we too have atrocities in our background.

The amount of conversation and debate that erupted on account of such statements was really quite striking. Clearly, people have ideas and opinions about what happened during the Crusades, and what we are to make of them. Anyone who has ever had to teach on the subject knows that this is one area of history that tends to spark passionate feelings, and that can make it decidedly difficult to talk about objectively. For example, many Protestants have a very negative view of the Crusades, and view them largely as the result of problematic Catholic doctrine and corruption within the Church hierarchy.

<!-- slide-break -->

While there are an exceedingly large number of things one would need to discuss to make a full analysis of the cause and effect of the Crusades (rather than only touching the tip of the iceberg, so to speak), we don't have the space here to do that sort of deep dive. Instead, we will predominantly be laying out evidence against a single point of view that we will argue is too simplistic: that the Crusades were a completely unprovoked attack on peaceable Muslims by bloodthirsty Christians who wanted land and loot. This is the sort of position typically put forward by those who wish to cast the entire affair in an overwhelmingly negative light, and make some point to the effect that Christians too have innocent blood on their hands. (At least if one buys that it is ever proper to blame the majority for the sins of the minority, which I do not).

Now, we aren't going to be arguing that there were absolutely no selfish motives in play here (to be frank, when is that *ever* true in history?), but merely discuss some historical context that sort of explodes the completely neat, tidy version of this particular narrative. In other words, we won't be pretending there is nothing to criticize on the Christian side, but we also won't be overly whitewashing the Muslim side (like is necessary in order to paint the Crusades in the one-sided overwhelmingly negative way some people do).

As in most other conflicts in history, when viewed from a neutral perspective, you can make a good case that nobody was completely innocent, and both sides had plenty of problems. That is the approach I take in analyzing the Crusades, at any rate.

<!-- slide-break -->

In the video, Dr. Reeves says this:

{{% quote work="The First Crusades (Part I)" workurl="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7bGxMcSHOmI" author="Ryan Reeves" authorurl="https://www.youtube.com/@RyanReevesM" %}}

The pope did not call The Crusade in an effort to conquer land; there was no effort in the West initially to move armies/vast numbers of troops at enormous expense in an effort to retake the Holy Land for the Western Church or for the papacy. When the pope called for the Crusade, he really only wanted an army of a few thousand to travel down there and to assist the Byzantine empire in their ongoing war and struggle to define the borders of their lands over against the [Seljuk Turks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seljuk_Empire).

So what's going on here is not a land grab or a money grab; it's not necessarily corrupt people initially saying that they are going to enact a war in an effort to steal money or land or any of these kinds of things, *per se*.

In fact the initial pact (the initial Crusader Oath) required that whatever lands were conquered---if they had been formerly Byzantine lands---that they would be handed back to the Byzantine emperor immediately.

Secondly and most importantly (this is the one that comes up more often than not)---the Crusades were *not* the first [sally](https://www.vocabulary.com/dictionary/sally%20forth) of the war or the struggle between the Middle East and the Western World.

{{% /quote %}}

<!-- slide-break -->

There are several points made here, as it seems to me:

1. That retaking the Holy Land was, at the least, not the only initial motivation for the Crusades. For example, there was also the matter of aiding Byzantine Christians in their conflict with the Muslim Seljuk Turks.
3. That the fact that Crusader Oath made knights yield conquered lands over to the Byzantine Empire shows that personal greed could not have played a role as much as some people say.
4. That this is not the first conflict between Christians and Muslims, and so must be analyzed in terms of all that came before.

I should note that how much of the First Crusade was motivated by coming to the aid of the Byzantine Empire (i.e., *defending* territorial borders) and how much by a desire to reconquer the Holy Land (i.e., *expanding* territorial borders) is actually rather controversial, as far as scholarly debates go. In [The Byzantine Background to the First Crusade](https://deremilitari.org/2013/06/the-byzantine-background-to-the-first-crusade/) (an excellent source for background on the Byzantine side of things), Paul Magadalino days this:

{{% quote work="The Byzantine Background to the First Crusade" workurl="https://deremilitari.org/2013/06/the-byzantine-background-to-the-first-crusade/" author="Paul Magadalino" authorurl="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Magdalino" %}}

There seems to be general agreement that Urban II would not have preached the crusade if Alexios I had not asked for help against the Turks.

{{% /quote %}}

<!-- slide-break -->

Unpacking this statement requires us to have a bit of background:

- [Byzantine emperor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_emperor) [Alexios I Comnenus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexius_I_Comnenus) sent envoys to the [Council of Piacenza](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Council_of_Piacenza) in March 1095, requesting military assistance against the [Seljuk Turks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seljuk_Turks), who were encroaching upon Byzantine lands.
- In November 1095, there was a different council called the [Council of Clermont](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Council_of_Clermont) called by [Pope Urban II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pope_Urban_II). The actual purpose of this council was actually pretty unrelated to the Crusades, but on the last day (November 27, 1095) Pope Urban II gave a famous speech that served as the call to arms that kicked off the First Crusade. This is viewed by most scholars as being in response to Alexios I's prior request for aid.

<!-- slide-break -->

As is ever the case in historical analysis, the sources we have that describe Pope Urban II's speech at Clermont do not all agree with each other. Excluding a letter about the council that Pope Urban II wrote himself, we have five main sources of information:

- The anonymous [*Gesta Francorum*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gesta_Francorum) ("The Deeds of the Franks" dated c. 1100/1101)
- [Fulcher of Chartres](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fulcher_of_Chartres), who was present at the council, in his [*Gesta Francorum Iherusalem peregrinantium*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gesta_Francorum_Iherusalem_peregrinantium) (c. 1100–1105)
- [Robert the Monk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_the_Monk), who may have been present at the council, in Historia Hierosolymitana (c. 1107)
- [Baldric, archbishop of Dol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baldric,_archbishop_of_Dol) (c. 1105)
- [Guibert de Nogent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guibert_de_Nogent), [*Dei gesta per Francos*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dei_gesta_per_Francos) (c. 1107/1108)

<!-- slide-break -->

According to [Dana Carleton Munro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dana_Carleton_Munro)'s [*The speech of Pope Urban II at Clermont*](https://catalog.hathitrust.org/Record/011212737/Home), all versions except the *Gesta Francorum* agree that Pope Urban II talked about:

1. The violence of European society and the necessity of maintaining the Peace of God
2. Helping the Greeks, who had asked for assistance
3. The crimes being committed against Christians in the East
4. A new kind of war, an armed pilgrimage, and of rewards in heaven, where remission of sins was offered to any who might die in the undertaking

Past these goals, Robert the Monk's account of Clermont also specifically mentions the reconquest of the Holy Land (i.e., on top of merely aiding the Greeks in their pre-existing conflict), which leads us to ask whether or not Pope Urban II actually spoke of the reconquest of Jerusalem right from the get-go. Not all the sources specifically mention Jerusalem as the ultimate goal, so it is hard to say with certainty. It doesn't help that all of these versions were written *after* Jerusalem had been captured in 1099, and it is therefore difficult to know what was actually said versus what was recreated in the aftermath of the successful crusade in a form of post-hoc retcon. Either way, it is clear that no matter how things started, the First Crusade clearly ended up targeting Jerusalem in fairly short order.

<!-- slide-break -->

Some scholars think that the papal goalposts were adjusted on a rolling basis based upon the growing popularity of the Crusade among the common people. That is, after the papacy saw the growing tide of popular support and realized they might be able to accomplish more than initially thought, they dynamically updated the public-facing messaging. This point of view is somewhat persuasive, insofar as the papacy seems to have wanted to recruit knights initially, but instead in practice ended up recruiting a bunch of peasants. For example, compare [Peter the Hermit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_the_Hermit) and the so-called ["People's Crusade"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Crusade#Peter_the_Hermit_and_the_People's_Crusade). Whatever the papacy's initial intentions, this popular movement clearly wanted to target Jerusalem specifically. So it is possible that the papacy decided to pivot to use this groundswell of popular support, rather than planning for it from the beginning. Although you might also argue that the papacy always intended to spark and then harness a wave of popular support, which is why Pope Urban II promised that anyone who participated would receive immediate remission of sins. That is quite the carrot to dangle, especially in context, given how most viewed forgiveness of sin at that point in time.

<!-- slide-break -->

So which is it---did the papacy pivot to go along with popular support, or intentionally manipulate public sentiment behind-the-scenes in order to bring about the outcome they planned beforehand? We needn't necessarily take a firm position here to accomplish our goal of arguing that the motivations behind the Crusades are objectively much less clear-cut than popular narratives often suggest (including the specific narrative that the Crusades were an act of unprovoked aggression on the part of greedy Christians who wanted land and loot). Rather than going down this path of trying to boil the Crusades down to neat categories of black and white that can serve to provoke emotional outrage (either for or against), in my opinion, it is much healthier as an academic approach to try to take a step back and come up with evidence-based explanatory models that make a good-faith effort to account for *all* data (not just cherry-picked data supporting only one side). This is much, much harder to do properly, and will necessarily involve speaking mostly in terms of probabilities and hypotheses (rather than moral absolutes). It also has the disadvantage of getting dry and technical rather quickly. But this path is the only one likely to avoid gross oversimplification.

<!-- slide-break -->

So what then will we argue sparked the First Crusade, if it was not merely a blatant land grab? Well, I think you go too far the other way if you try to argue that the First Crusade was limited in scope to just being a defensive war to support the Byzantine Empire's borders against the encroaching Seljuk Turks. Those circumstances are certainly related to the launching of the First Crusade (and we shouldn't try to pretend otherwise), but I don't think it is appropriate to put 100% of the focus there either, as if that was all that was going on here.

Rather, the reasons for the First Crusade seem to be decidedly complex and multifaceted. In my opinion, [this Reddit post](https://www.reddit.com/r/AskHistorians/comments/1k3m8un/comment/mo3veam/) on [r/AskHistorians](https://www.reddit.com/r/AskHistorians/) does a good job explaining some of the [*casus belli*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Casus_belli), without weighting any one factor higher than is appropriate:

<!-- slide-break -->

{{% quote work="Is there any merit in the theory that the First Crusade started because Muslims were oppressing Christians in the Levant and preventing pilgrimages to Jerusalem?" workurl="https://www.reddit.com/r/AskHistorians/comments/1k3m8un/comment/mo3veam/" author="jogarz" authorurl="https://www.reddit.com/user/jogarz/" %}}

In short, yes, there is merit in stating that the First Crusade was in part a response to Muslim violence against Christians. However, in part is a key qualifier here. There were several motivating factors for the Crusade. To briefly describe some of the other motivations:

- A desire to liberate the physical holy places, distinct from their Christian inhabitants. Jerusalem looked large in the thoughts of Western Christians at the time, and the idea of the [Church of the Holy Sepulcher](https://www.reddit.com/search/?q=Church+of+the+Holy+Sepulcher+First+Crusade&cId=074a2485-f01b-4d78-b7cf-ea9704060641&iId=a64676be-441c-4aa3-8bc4-74f806a4f05a) languishing in Muslim hands was seen as a humiliation and insult to Christians.
- A desire to reunite the Western and Eastern churches. The Papacy hoped that by assisting the Byzantines against their enemies, the Great Schism could eventually be healed and the universal church could be reunified.
<!-- slide-break -->
- A desire to strengthen Papal authority in Western Europe. The First Crusade took place at the height of the [Gregorian Reform movement](https://www.reddit.com/search/?q=Gregorian+Reform+movement+Papal+authority&cId=79f3c689-c4f5-4aca-8bd9-b49c64b72664&iId=f0b7d1c3-e735-4acc-9072-a69913a49dfc), which sought to purify corruption in the Church, in part by limiting secular influence over its affairs. This meant strengthening the power of the Papacy. By calling for and organizing such a momentous endeavor as the Crusade, the Popes could further their image as the paramount leaders of Western Christendom.
- A desire to provide an outlet for the violence endemic in Western Europe at the time. Ironically, the Crusades were directly connected to the 10th and 11th century European peace movement, which sought to limit the petty wars waged by the nobility and their knights. A common theme in Crusade preaching was that warriors should stop their sinful, selfish violence against their fellow Christians at home, and instead direct their energies towards a righteous war beyond the sea.

No single factor has complete explanatory power, and all played a role in creating the conditions that led to the First Crusade. So while it is valid to state that violence and oppression towards Christians motivated the First Crusade, it is important to remember that this wasn’t the *only* motivation for the Crusades.

{{% /quote %}}

<!-- slide-break -->

It would also be remiss of me not to mention here that as I understand it, there is actually a good bit of disagreement in the academy about how to characterize the Crusades, with some modern scholarly takes essentially breaking completely with past views on the subject. To quote [The Byzantine Background to the First Crusade](https://deremilitari.org/2013/06/the-byzantine-background-to-the-first-crusade/) again:

{{% quote work="The Byzantine Background to the First Crusade" workurl="https://deremilitari.org/2013/06/the-byzantine-background-to-the-first-crusade/" author="Paul Magadalino" authorurl="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Magdalino" %}}

Fortunately for those of us who are not historians of the crusades, there is no lack of up-to-date literature to help us concentrate our minds. Indeed, the last thirty-five years have seen a remarkable flowering of crusade scholarship in North America, in Israel, in Germany, and above all in Britain, where a generation of productive and dedicated scholars, mainly under the leadership of Jonathan Riley-Smith, is rewriting the whole history of the crusading movement, in practice and in theory, from beginning to end. They have already published significant contributions to the re-interpretation of the First Crusade – I mention Riley-Smith’s own book on the subject, published in 1986, John France’s military history of the crusade, and Marcus Bull’s study of lay piety in south western France in the eleventh century – and we can no doubt look forward to the appearance of more volumes occasioned by the ninth centenary celebrations. The day is hopefully not far off when we will know all that can be known, or at least will have heard all that can usefully be said, about what was in Pope Urban’s mind when he preached at Clermont, and what was in the minds of the laymen who answered, or did not answer, his call to arms. What is certain is that the labours of so many energetic *milites Christi* make it unnecessary, and indeed impertinent, for a Byzantinist to pronounce on such matters. We have come a long way since the 1950s, when a Byzantinist, Sir Steven Runciman, could write an authoritative, best-selling history of the crusades.

<!-- slide-break -->

And yet, in moving on from Runciman, we have lost as well as gained. We have lost not only the combination of scholarly erudition and narrative ease which still make Runciman an unbeatable first introduction to the subject; we have also lost the sense of Byzantium as something integral to the crusading movement. Whereas Runciman stressed the role of the Byzantine emperor Alexios I (1081-1118) in initiating the crusade, directing the crusaders and helping them on their way, the two recent monographs by Jonathan Riley-Smith and John France have cast him as a much more marginal player. Riley-Smith suggests that relations with Byzantium were not uppermost in Urban II’s calculations, and France, after dismissing the evidence of the main Byzantine source, the biography of Alexios I by his daughter Anna Comnena, argues that Alexius did little to help the crusade and was indifferent to its fate once he had used it to recapture Nicaea. Neither of these judgements is highly momentous in itself, but taken together and in the context of the general drift of modern crusade scholarship, they can be regarded as symptomatic of a tendency to write Byzantium out of the script, or, to choose a metaphor appropriate to our small academic world of conferences and symposia, not to invite Byzantium to the party. Byzantium is barely mentioned in the *Oxford History of the Crusades*, edited by Jonathan Riley-Smith and published in 1995. Byzantium thus comes to be seen as an unwilling, passive and even obstructive channel that the crusade had to pass through in order to get from Western Europe to the Holy Land: the place that happened to be on the way, or in the way. To use another metaphor, Byzantium was the temporary conductor of an electrical charge which the empire played no part in creating and had no interest in retaining. The implications of the fact that it received the current because its own battery needed recharging are not even considered.

{{% /quote %}}

<!-- slide-break -->

In other words, the way in which certain more recent scholars like [Jonathan Riley-Smith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jonathan_Riley-Smith) have approached the Crusades is very different from some older sources like [Steven Runciman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steven_Runciman). I am not here taking a position on which view to hold, so much as noting that there are in fact views plural to pick between, rather than one dominant paradigm that is universally held.

While I certainly cannot speak for him, parts of what Dr. Reeves says in the video seem to lean towards some of Riley-Smith's views, which hold that the Crusades were not really driven by a desire for material gain, *pace* popular narratives. Compare again Dr. Reeves's statement that I previously quoted: "[W]hat's going on here is not a land grab or a money grab; it's not necessarily corrupt people initially saying that they are going to enact a war in an effort to steal money or land or any of these kinds of things."

<!-- slide-break -->

In fact, you cannot discuss the Crusades in a vacuum, ignoring the hundreds of years of prior conflicts between Christians and Muslims (including many with the Muslim side being the aggressors, to be clear). Put simply, trying to blame the Crusades for all Islamic hostility towards Christianity is misguided at best, and outright propagandistic at worst. This notion that there were all these peaceful Muslims out there minding there own business until bloodthirsty Christians showed up and burned everything down (and only then did Muslims start fighting Christians, making the origin of the religious conflict between these two groups rest solely upon the shoulders of Christians) is plain fiction.

Ever since its conception, it was not uncommon for Islam to expand more through the sword than the pen, particularly when conquering the lands of those with markedly different cultures (like, say, Christians). Was it always bloody wars of conquest? Of course not. But it certainly was sometimes. Regardless of where exactly one draws the lines in categorizing specific events in the expansion of Islam, the point is that by the time you get to the Crusades, there was in fact plenty of Christian blood on Muslim hands. This does not necessarily justify what came after (since vengeance is to be the Lord's; cf. Romans 12:19). But it certainly *does* mean that the narrative articulated above about absolutely peaceful Muslims and completely bloodthirsty Christians is mostly nonsense.

<!-- slide-break -->

Rather, the Crusades merely mark the latest in a series of ongoing struggles between Christendom and Islam. Out West, there was the Muslim conquest of Spain, and their attempted expansion northwards and eastwards into parts of southern France (cf. the [Battle of Tours](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Tours)). Out East, the Byzantine Christians had been having land disputes with various Muslim political structures for many centuries. This is not to say that the two sides were never able to peacefully coexist (since there was plenty of that too in history); the point is, the Crusades were far from the first time the two religions had crossed blades, so to speak.

And in fact, the Crusades were far from the last time too. Frankly, all the way up until as late as the 16th century, European forces and Byzantine forces clashed with various Muslim states almost constantly.

So while the Crusades are indeed an enormously important part of the history of Christian-Muslim relations, they are not the only part of the story.

<!-- slide-break -->

#### Follow-on topics {#follow-on-topics}

##### Should we as Christians care about specific places, like Jerusalem? No, we should not

{{< properties

stags="Church > Is not a human institution or physical building, but is based upon saving faith in Jesus Christ | Church > Greek and Hebrew vocabulary related to the English translation of the word “church” | Seeking the Kingdom of God > Our citizenship is in heaven, not of this world | In the world, but not of the world"

ptags="Hebrews 11:13-16; Galatians 4:8-11"

>}}


Whether or not the papacy initially intended for the Crusades to reconquer Jerusalem, that goal quickly got adopted in practice. It is no straw man to say that the Christian propaganda of the time did clearly paint the military expeditions as a form of holy war to once again take control of the Holy Land. (To be fair, historical Muslims also frequently justified wars using religion, although we cannot be overly anachronistic in this, since many singularly militaristic views on [jihad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jihad) are in fact quite modern).

So, to put it bluntly, is there anything valid about this? That is, with respect to so-called [philosophy of war](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/war/), is "this place is holy to my religion!" a valid justification *jus ad bellum*?

The answer for Christians is no, biblically speaking, and I don't even view this is as my personal opinion, but as a biblical fact. It might seem odd for me to be so aggressive in emphasizing this, but it is quite necessary, since plenty of religious conflicts right up until the present involve this matter, and lots of people seem to get this wrong.

<!-- slide-break -->

Now, we need to be extremely careful how we talk about things here. For example:

- God did tell the people of Israel to conquer [Canaan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canaan), and treat the covenantal [Promised Land](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Promised_Land) He gave to them as something special and important.
- Lots of stuff in the Law required going to *the* (definite article) temple in Jerusalem.
- During the Babylonian exile (for example), we know that the longing Jews had for their homeland was entirely proper and godly.
- Geographic Israel will play an important role in eschatology.
- Etc.

So the question is *not* "Does God ever want us to treat some specific place as special?" but "Does God *right now* (i.e., current dispensation) want us to treat some specific place as special?" Those are two different matters entirely.

Old Testament Jews living under the Law clearly were supposed to treat Jerusalem and the temple therein with great reverence. But we are not Old Testament Jews living under the Law. Moreover, that version of Jerusalem entirely ceased to exist after 70 A.D., when Titus essentially razed the city to the ground (including the temple).

<!-- slide-break -->

Unlike the Law---which involved many physical rituals---the new covenant of grace is mostly inward not outward. Moreover, Jesus's kingdom is not of this world (John 18:36); our homeland is not a place upon earth, but in heaven:

{{% scripture p="Hebrews 11:13-16 | ESV" %}}

**13** These [godly believers] all died in faith, not having received the things promised, but having seen them and greeted them from afar, and having acknowledged that they were strangers and exiles on the earth. **14** For people who speak thus make it clear that they are seeking a homeland. **15** If they had been thinking of that land from which they had gone out, they would have had opportunity to return. **16** But as it is, they desire a better country, that is, a heavenly one. Therefore God is not ashamed hto be called their God, for he has prepared for them a city.

{{% /scripture %}}

How ironic it is then that Christians throughout history fought wars over physical Jerusalem! They ought to have instead turned their eyes towards heavenly Jerusalem.

<!-- slide-break -->

What is even worse is that Christians have taken an obsession with the physical far further than just Jerusalem (which at least has going for it that it *used to be* a place of proper focus for believers, and will be again in the future). For example, plenty of Christians act like cathedrals and church buildings are holy. They are not. They are just buildings. What have buildings to do with our faith, or the teaching of the Word of God? Those are the important things, but they are not physical.

It is in fact blasphemous to think God specially dwells in things built by human hands; He only did that (insofar as one can call a symbolic physical column of cloud "doing that") with the tabernacle and then the temple. But now *we* are the temple (1 Peter 2:4-5), indwelt by the Holy Spirit. Focusing on physical buildings is therefore emphasizing exactly the wrong thing.

If the Vatican were to get wiped off the face of of the earth tomorrow, nothing changes. If the physical local church we attend were to get wiped off the face of the earth tomorrow, *nothing changes*. Why? Because the church is us, the assembly of human beings, not the building. After all, that is what the word "church" (Greek: [ἐκκλησία](https://biblehub.com/greek/1577.htm)) actually means.

<!-- slide-break -->

To take things even further, imagine for a moment that traditionally Christian regions (e.g., Western Europe, America) were to get conquered by theocratic Muslim regimes. What ought we make of that? Well, are we American first, or Christian first? Is our loyalty first to God, or to the physical land? You may scoff, but lots of nations throughout history have completely ceased to exist, and we see all too many examples of people who compromise themselves fighting for a country that is physical not spiritual. We are *not* citizens of this world, but are citizens of heaven.

<!-- slide-break -->

So when we read in history of Christians going on physical pilgrimages to supposedly holy places (and even going to war to support such pilgrimages), we should shake our heads, and have the same attitude as Paul did with the Galatians:

{{% scripture p="Galatians 4:8-11 | NIV11" %}}

**8** Formerly, when you did not know God, you were slaves to those who by nature are not gods. **9** But now that you know God—or rather are known by God—how is it that you are turning back to those weak and miserable forces? #### Summary points {#summary-points}

We will be arguing that a crusade was really a combination of several different concepts sort of stitched together:

1. A plenary indulgence
2. A pilgrimage to the Holy Land
3. A holy war to defend the faith

Around the time of the First Crusade, several different concepts sort of coalesced and came together, a set of convictions and principles that were to define the crusading era. Rather than these things somehow themselves launching the First Crusade, it is more like the definition of what exactly a crusade even was sort of developed alongside the First Crusade itself.

So when Pope Urban II gave his call to arms at Clermont in 1095, at that time those hearing him would not have had a fleshed-out conception of what precisely this endeavor would be. But as the First Crusade achieves some measure of success---as it self-identifies as a crusade---then a sort of *de facto* definition gets built on the fly. This means the First Crusade in essence caused the concept of crusading to gain a firm definition; a pre-existing firm definition did not cause the First Crusade. It is important for us to not flip the order here when we think about things, since getting it backwards can warp our view of how the First Crusade developed.

<!-- slide-break -->

In terms of doctrinal issues in play, one important thing was the [plenary indulgence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indulgence#Plenary_indulgences) offered to crusaders. In Catholic theology, an [indulgence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indulgence) is some way to reduce the temporal punishment for forgiven sin (i.e., sin that has been formally confessed to a priest). In their understanding, the process of sanctification requires not only forgiveness from the guilt (*culpa*) of sin, but also purification from the harmful effects or wounds of sin.

To use video game terminology, even after absolution is granted by a priest during confession, you could say that the penitent are still under a "negative consequences of sin" debuff. This debuff even persists after death; in Catholic theology, [purgatory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Purgatory) exists so that this debuff (i.e., the temporal punishment of sin) can be fully cleansed before eternity. (Purgatory is seen as being still essentially "temporal").

<!-- slide-break -->

In Catholic theology, there are two basic ways the debuff gets removed: 1) penance, and 2) indulgences. The mechanics here are very different.

Penance is:

1. Assigned by a priest during confession; Catholic priests assign [sacramental penance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Penance#Sacrament_of_Penance) as part of the [Catholic Sacrament of Reconciliation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Penance_in_the_Catholic_Church), before they give [absolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Absolution#Catholic_Church).
2. Obligatory/mandatory
3. Related to helping remove the debuff for a specific sin (i.e., something that was just confessed). Penance is therefore limited in scope.

Indulgences are:

1. Pursued electively. The recipient of an indulgence can choose to perform some action to receive it, but does not "have" to in order receive absolution through confession.
2. Completely optional
3. Related to removing the debuff for all already-confessed sins generally. Indulgences are therefore broad in scope.

<!-- slide-break -->

Another difference is that indulgences can be applied to the debuff of someone other than yourself: in Catholic teaching, indulgences can be applied to the dead in purgatory, but they cannot be applied to other living people on earth.

There are two types of indulgences: [plenary indulgences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indulgence#Plenary_indulgences) (cf. the Latin adjective [*plenus*](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/plenus), meaning "full" or "complete") completely remove the debuff across all past sins, while [partial indulgences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indulgence#Partial_indulgences) only partially remove the debuff across all past sins. This means plenary indulgences are "more powerful".

<!-- slide-break -->

What I have just gone through is a general summary of current Catholic teachings on these things, just to define terms and introduce the concepts. What I am going to do now is discuss some history related to indulgences.

Hand-waving a bit:

- Plenary indulgences were extremely rare and a massive deal in early medieval history. Pope Urban II's speech at the Council of Clermont more or less directly stated that participation in the the First Crusade would serve as a plenary indulgence. This is widely seen as being the first official plenary indulgence in Catholic church history.
- During most of the High Middle Ages, plenary indulgences were practically unheard of outside of going on a crusade.
- But by the late High Middle Ages, the Church allowed people to gain them by proxy---such as paying for a substitute fighter or funding a crusade if you were too old or sick to go.
- Non-crusade plenary indulgences only began later (cf. the [First Roman Jubilee in 1300](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jubilee_in_the_Catholic_Church#First_Christian_jubilee)), and widespread commercialization and abuses did not peak until the 15th century. The blatant selling of indulgences was one of the things that brought about the [Protestant Reformation](https://simple.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protestant_Reformation).

<!-- slide-break -->

To understand why tens of thousands of people trekked thousands of miles eastwards from Western Europe in the First Crusade, you have to put in context how big a deal this plenary indulgence was seen to be at the time. Medieval Christians *deeply* feared purgatory and the debt of temporal punishment left after sins were confessed. But Pope Urban II said that you could wipe out that entire debt instantly by going on a crusade. That was a powerful motivator indeed!

So this forms the first part of our definition of a crusade:

> A crusade was:
>
> 1. A plenary indulgence

<!-- slide-break -->

That brings us to the second concept involved here: the idea of going on a pilgrimage.

The idea of pilgrimages dates back to the very early church (which, in my opinion, is good evidence demonstrating how quickly the church visible went off the rails after the passing of the Apostles). Christians would basically go on trips to various locations (often those associated with the life of Christ). These trips were considered "good works", meaning in practice that they often ended up intertwined with penance and indulgences (see above). The Holy Land in particular was seen as being especially meaningful as a target for pilgrimage.

This forms the second part of our definition of a crusade:

> A crusade was:
>
> 1. A plenary indulgence
> 2. A pilgrimage to the Holy Land

<!-- slide-break -->

And finally we arrive at the last big idea: the notion of a holy war. This is perhaps the hardest piece to define precisely, since there are a number of moving pieces.

First off, medieval Europe had a problem on its hands when it came to violence and war. The church was trying very hard to stamp out conflicts between feudal lords, and part of that was by instituting moral codes for warriors. Chivalry, in some senses, can be viewed as a propaganda effort on the part of the church to sidestep nobility and directly control knights. The problem is that moralizing to knights does not make them stop being warriors. Put differently, men who train very hard for years to gain experience in the art of war will not put down their swords just because you ask nicely and tell them to please stop fighting. 

Compared to moralizing alone, the church calling a crusade was much more effective overall in eliminating knight-driven violence in Europe. This is so for a couple big reasons:

1. It let squabbling feudal kingdoms unite against a perceived common enemy.
2. It gave knights a way to engage in war without having to make restitution or justification for it. In fact, far from needing to undertake penance for their violence, they would now be receiving a plenary indulgence for the same. Clearly, that was received by them much more favorably.

<!-- slide-break -->

In terms of dressing this up prettily so people would actually buy it, the papacy leaned heavily on concepts related to so-called ["just war"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Just_war_theory), first put forward centuries earlier by theologians like [Ambrose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Just_war_theory#Saint_Ambrose) and [Augustine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Just_war_theory#Saint_Augustine). Pope Urban II may not have directly quoted Augustine in his Clermont speech (i.e., to outline a formal theory of just war to rationalize the First Crusade), but all the basic elements were there nonetheless.

{{% note %}}

Eventually moral justification for the crusades was formalized more explicitly by canon jurists. For example, around 1140, [Gratian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decretum_Gratiani#Author) compiled a manuscript known as the [*Decretum Gratiani*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decretum_Gratiani). In *Causa* 23, he heavily quotes Saint Augustine to build a formal just war theory. For more, see:

- [Just War and Crusades (Chapter 28) - The Cambridge History of Medieval Canon Law](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/cambridge-history-of-medieval-canon-law/just-war-and-crusades/8C78979F9E5A6D07D5EE89FF3F546E07)

{{% /note %}}

<!-- slide-break -->

This approach was quite a bit more hawkish than earlier Christian views on war, which tended more towards complete pacificism. But by casting the affair not as a war of aggression, but as as a moral fight to defend righteousness, taking up arms to defend the faith became inextricably linked to the crusader spirit. And so it was that the notion of "holy war" came to be an essential part of what the crusades were in practice.

This forms the third and final part of our definition of a crusade:

> A crusade was:
>
> 1. A plenary indulgence
> 2. A pilgrimage to the Holy Land
> 3. A holy war to defend the faith

#### Follow-on topics

##### Avoiding straw men in discussing penance and indulgences

{{< properties

stags="Apologetics > Avoid misrepresenting the positions of others"

>}}

Oftentimes, Protestants partially or totally straw man the Catholic position on penance and indulgences, and say that this is directly teaching salvation by works. However, the Catholic position is not precisely that penance and indulgences forgive sin (in the sense of removing guilt, which even in their view is only removed by the blood of Christ), but that these things are necessary to remove the negative consequences of sin in practice.

We may disagree with this as a position, but we should make sure that at least this is what we are arguing against, rather than a misrepresentation of their position.

<!-- slide-break -->

In my opinion, one of the best arguments against penance and indulgences is that this passage mentions no way to mitigate God's discipline, nor does trying to do that even make particularly good sense if the purpose of God's discipline is to grow us. That is to say, if the purpose of discipline from the hand of our loving Father is to teach us, then seeking to completely sidestep it is somewhat counterproductive, is it not? Even after we confess and are forgiven (cf. 1 John 1:9), we may still face consequences. But this is entirely for our own good!

There is also the fact that this passage clearly teaches that discipline comes from God Himself. That would seem to be obvious, but it bears repeating, since Catholic doctrine teaches that priests assign penance. Interesting that Hebrews 12 doesn't mention such a practice at all, is it not?

<!-- slide-break -->

Also, more broadly, consider the training that the discipline of the Lord is supposed to effect in us. Because God is a perfect Father, His discipline always teaches us perfectly. Whatever area we need to be taught a lesson in (be it pride, lust, anger, or whatever else), God's discipline will effectively train us in that area.

As I see things, the sledgehammer nature of indulgences would seem to inherently prevent such tailored training in righteousness. If I can get rid of the negative consequences of my lust by going on a pilgrimage to a specific place, well, what precisely does the latter have to do with the former? You can try to wave your hand at it and say that the good works of penance and indulgences help reduce our attachment to the world and so on (and that that is how the negative consequences of sin are minimized in practice), but to me, this is much less intuitive, and seems needlessly indirect.

As a human parent, if I am punishing my young son for trying to touch a hot stove, I am not going to stop lecturing him about the hot stove just because he did something else right (like doing well on a test, for example). How then will he effectively get the lesson about the hot stove?

So if we can see this as human parents, why would we think God works any differently? What is actually best for us is getting the entirety of the lesson (through God's perfect tailored discipline)... even if we did something else good in the meantime.

##### The fact that the Catholic church hierarchy directly controls penance and indulgences (not to mention absolution) gives it inherent power over those who believe in these doctrines

{{< properties

stags=""

>}}

I should point out that this is not itself a moral condemnation of the Catholic church. After all, if the Catholic church is in fact God's chosen institution upon earth (as they claim), it having a lot of power is not necessarily a bad thing. But nevertheless, the fact that:

1. Priests have the power to gatekeep absolution behind mandatory acts of penance (and priests are controlled by the central church hierarchy)
2. The church higher-ups are the only one who can define indulgences

Means that the central church has an enormous amount of power over those who buy into these things... like the crusaders.

<!-- slide-break -->

Think about it. According to Catholic theology, temporal punishment for sin (even after you confess it to a priest) might lead to Bad Things in time, or even land you in purgatory. And the only way to avoid said Bad Things and/or purgatory is to do what the church tells you; they are the ones who set the rules. No matter how you look at it, the church now has complete control over you.

It even gets worse, because according to Catholic doctrine, you need a priest in order to receive absolution; you cannot do it on your own. That is to say, if you are not willing to bow the knee to central church authority (since they are the ones who ultimately control the priests), you cannot even receive forgiveness at all.

Of course, most of us Protestants reject all of this. We believe that confession should be directly to God (1 John 1:9), and after confession/genuine repentance, that is it. Under this system of belief, there is no mechanism for my pastor to inject himself into the forgiveness of sin. Even if he wanted to, he cannot weaponize fear and guilt to control me (e.g., by directly stating or even just implying that I have to XYZ in order to not face Bad Things on account of my sin).

The question thus becomes whether or not the centralized Catholic church has ever actively used penance and indulgences to exploit fear and guilt to manipulate people. Given that I am a non-denominational Protestant and have never hidden my distaste for centralized church authority structures, you will no doubt be unsurprised to hear that I believe the answer to this is very much yes.

<!-- slide-break -->

Some things I think should also be considered when examining these doctrines critically:

- In the beginning, apparently the only way you could get a plenary indulgence was by yourself going on a crusade. Then it changed to maybe paying for someone else to go on the crusade on your behalf, if you could not do it yourself for some reason. Fast forward many centuries, and now there are all sorts of ways people can receive a plenary indulgence that are *far* simpler than going on a crusade. In fact, modern Catholic teachings allow people to receive a plenary indulgence once daily. So let me ask, did God suddenly start treating sin much more casually? On what basis can modern people do objectively trivial things to receive full remittance of sin, whereas medieval people had to risk their lives for the same? You can wave your hand and say that modern popes have, out of mercy and benevolence, just opened more paths to achieve full remittance of sin, but I submit that while using the papal authority/*ex cathedra* card might "work" if you presuppose your own position, the "cheapness" of modern plenary indulgences (compared to the early plenary indulgences that required going on crusades) should cause anybody with a remotely open mind to seriously question these teachings.
- Only in the 1960s did the Catholic church stop specifically quantifying partial indulgences in terms of "days/weeks/months/years of canonical penance equivalent". I am not straw manning; they really put specific number values on things. Of course, before this, they already had fixed numbers within penitential codes for penance, so I suppose quantifying indulgences too ought not be surprising on balance. I find both practices to be wholly arbitrary.
<!-- slide-break -->
- Of course, no discussion about indulgences would be complete without mentioning how the blatant selling of indulgences [played no small part in triggering the Protestant Reformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indulgence#Protestant_Reformation). One cannot get around the fact that this is established historical fact. Of course, Catholics have plenty of explanations for how the centralized Catholic church can apparently go astray here and there without it ultimately losing its authority, but taking a step back, I think we ought to seriously question the usefulness of trusting an institution that can be so blatantly corrupt. That is, if we accept that the Catholic church was terribly wrong at that point and time in history, what stops it from being terribly wrong about some other matter in the present? Nothing makes that logically impossible. To me, that is a very good reason to not put your trust in the centralized church, and to instead only trust in the Bible.

#### Summary points {#summary-points}

Out in in the East, as various Muslim empires rose and fell, there arose a division between [Sunni Islam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunni_Islam) and [Shia Islam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shia_Islam). This meant that to some extent there was not as much unity on the part of Muslims as their might have otherwise been, and this proved fortunate to the Byzantine Empire, in that they faced relatively smaller and more fragmented armies, which let them resist more effectively.

Nonetheless, the Byzantine world did at times lose land to the Muslims (or rather, the borders went back and forth [over hundreds of years of conflicts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab%E2%80%93Byzantine_wars))---at one point to the extent that [Constantinople itself was at risk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab%E2%80%93Byzantine_wars#Arab_attacks_on_Anatolia_and_sieges_of_Constantinople).

But by the early part of the 11th century, the Byzantine Empire found themselves having restored and even extended many of their eastern borders.

<!-- slide-break -->

But then the [Seljuks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seljuk_Empire) came on the scene (triggering the [Byzantine–Seljuk wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine%E2%80%93Seljuk_wars)), and things started going more poorly for the Byzantine Empire. Now, the Seljuks were an interesting group. They actually came from nomadic Turkic tribes from the steppes of Central Asia who moved south and converted to Islam. This means the Seljuks were *not* Arab, and that is important in understanding their society and culture, in that there were definite differences compared to the Arab Caliphates (like the [Rashidun Caliphate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rashidun_Caliphate), [Umayyad Caliphate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Umayyad_Caliphate), and [Abbasid Caliphate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abbasid_Caliphate), for example).

At any rate, a crushing strategic defeat of the Byzantine Empire by the Seljuk Empire in the 1071 [Battle of Manzikert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Manzikert) opened to the door to the Turkification of [Anatolia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anatolia), and a crumbling of the Byzantine Empire's ability to maintain its borders. The eastern emperor at the time ([Romanos IV Diogenes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanos_IV_Diogenes)) was even captured alive by the Seljuks.

<!-- slide-break -->

Of course, it was not just conflicts out east that set the backdrop for the Crusades. The Great Schism between the Eastern Church and Western Church (which we discussed previously [here](https://www.bibledocs.org/discussion/ryan-reeves/longer-topical-studies/early-and-medieval-history/great-schism-1054/)) happened in 1054. At the time the First Crusade rolled around in the 1090s, the schism was still only several decades old, and this close proximity meant that there was at that point a proportionally much higher percentage of people who though that reunification was inevitable, and just a matter of time. That sentiment clearly colored relations between East and West. In fact, arguably, you can see this attitude in action in [Alexios I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexius_I_Komnenos) sending his embassy to [Pope Urban II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pope_Urban_II) to request aid against the Seljuks. Basically, why would he bother reaching out to the West if there was not at least some hope of reconciliation? (I suppose one could argue that desperate times call for desperate measures).

<!-- slide-break -->

Whatever the case, this brings us to Pope Urban II's speech at Clermont that we already analyzed extensively in a previous section ([§How should we characterize the Crusades, at a high level?](#how-should-we-characterize-the-crusades-at-a-high-level)). (Relative to Dr. Reeves sequence in the video, I covered Clermont a bit earlier, since I thought it is quite important to bring up when going over potential Christian motivations for the First Crusade). Recapping some of the main points:

- On November 27, 1095 Pope Urban gave a speech at the [Council of Clermont](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Council_of_Clermont) (which was a church council that was initially about more or less unrelated things) that kicked off the First Crusade. Many scholars think that Alexios I's appeal is what ultimately led to this speech.
- All the main historical accounts of Clermont we have were written down after the First Crusade itself, so it is hard to separate myth from fact here.
- Pope Urban II demonizes the Seljuks in this speech, painting them as bloodthirsty savages persecuting Christians left and right.

<!-- slide-break -->

The initial papal call to arms (which was perhaps intended to recruit a few thousand people to go help the Byzantines in their conflict with the Seljuks) was quickly supplemented by lay preachers who sort of took the ball and ran with it. We just previously talked about how the people of the time self-defined crusades ([§What exactly was a crusade? Some combination of plenary indulgence, pilgrimage, and holy war](#what-exactly-was-a-crusade-some-combination-of-plenary-indulgence-pilgrimage-and-holy-war)), and many of these views were shaped by the popular movement that took on something of a life of its own.

Whatever people's individual reasons (since the reasons did no doubt vary to at least some degree across individuals), the overall marketing proved effective, for in the end, more than 100,000 people ended up embarking on the First Crusade. Even today a mobilization of 100,000 people is a lot, but how much more so back then when populations were much smaller on the whole!

#### Summary points {#summary-points}

The first mobilization of the First Crusade somewhat surprisingly did not actually involve a sortie by an army of professional soldiers. Instead it was a rather rag-tag group of peasants that first set out East. This band of more-or-less untrained people (often called the [People's Crusade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/People%27s_Crusade)) set out in Spring 1096, whereas the professional armies (often called the Princes' Crusade) did not set out until Late Summer 1096.

Now, if the professional Byzantine armies fell to the military might of the Seljuks, how much more so the People's Crusade. A bunch of mostly untrained peasants attacking the well-trained Seljuk army calls to mind a quote from the ancient Chinese text [Mozi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mozi): "To refute my principle with one's own principle is like throwing an egg against a boulder. The eggs in the world would be exhausted without doing any harm to the boulder." (The wider idiom this quote spawned---[以卵投石](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E4%BB%A5%E5%8D%B5%E6%8A%95%E7%9F%B3) (pinyin: *yǐluǎntóushí*), literally, "throwing an egg against a boulder"---essentially means "to overestimate one's capabilities or to attack a stronger opponent as the weaker side, which is sure to end in failure"). Here, the Seljuk army is the boulder, and the peasant army is the egg. The clash between the two went exactly as one would expect: in the battle of [Battle of Civetot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Civetot) most of the individuals making up the People's Crusade got completely slaughtered. Calling it a battle is even giving them more credit than one probably should, particularly since the crusaders weren't even properly unified at this point in time (since they splintered across ethnic lines and started squabbling basically as soon as they actually reached Anatolia...).

<!-- slide-break -->

This entire affair might seem eminently irrational... and it really was, as hindsight clearly shows. That then leaves us as students of history with the problem of figuring out what exactly was going through these people's heads at the time. What would cause them to think that running on ahead of the professional armies was even remotely a good idea?

Were they just crazy? If what you mean by that is diagnosable mental illness, then probably not. But if you mean brainwashed by delusional end-of-the-world drivel, then yeah, at least some of them were certifiably crazy.

Humans seem to have a morbid fascination with the end of the world, and it has the unfortunate tendency to induce utterly nonsensical behavior in "true believers". This is true in modern times (cf. the [mass suicide at Jonestown in 1978](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jonestown#Mass_murder%E2%80%93suicide), the tragic true origin of the idiom "drink the Kool-Aid"), and it has also been true all throughout history, including here with the People's Crusade.

As best we can tell, there was a fervent [apocalyptic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apocalypticism)/[Millenarian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Millenarianism) sentiment simmering during this period, stoked by some of the leaders of the People's Crusade like [Peter the Hermit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_the_Hermit) (who we discussed before), as well as [Volkmar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volkmar_%28crusader%29) and [Emicho](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emicho_%28crusader%29). The thought seems to have been that if they successfully marched on Jerusalem, they could bring in the second coming of Christ and begin the end times struggle against Antichrist, or something to that effect.

<!-- slide-break -->

So how did this sort of sentiment find a foothold in the minds of the common people? Well, severe droughts, famines, pestilence, and economic collapse across Europe in 1095 created an undeniably apocalyptic mood:

- 1095 began with a brutally severe winter. There were massive snowstorms that killed multitudes of livestock and people. Rivers across Europe froze over, and subsequent thaws caused floods that at times swept away critical bridges.
- Following the severe winter, Europe experienced an intense, suffocating summer heatwave. The extreme temperatures and resulting droughts directly killed large numbers of people.
- At least partially as a result of the extreme weather patterns, there were successive harvest failures in 1095 that left grain reserves empty, inflating food prices and starving rural and urban populations.
- Driven by the consecutive blows of freezing winter floods, severe drought, and failing crops, a widespread pestilence and famine swept through Europe in 1095.
  - At least some of the pestilence was a horrific gangrenous illness known as [ergotism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ergotism), which rotted limbs and caused agonizing deaths.
- Widespread economic despair, hunger, and death eroded normal societal bounds, making the common folk desperate for divine intervention.

In the midst of all this, there was a massive meteor shower in April 1095. The awe-inspiring celestial phenomena of falling stars was seen as a divine sign by some (cf. the statements made by [Gilbert of Lisieux](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gilbert_de_Magminot)).

All these things together are hypothesized to be the reason why people at the time were willing to seriously entertain thoughts that this really might be the end.

<!-- slide-break -->

{{% note %}}

The apocalyptic spirit was not limited to *just* the People's Crusade (although it was certainly well-represented there). How do we know this? Well, even after the slaughter of the People's Crusade in October 1096 at the Battle of Civetot, some celestial phenomena were still taken by the crusaders as having spiritual import. For example, around the time of the [Siege of Antioch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Antioch):

- In early October 1097, just before the crusaders' Siege of Antioch began, a bright comet with a tail shaped like a sword was seen in the sky. The crusaders viewed this celestial event as a divine omen and a sign of God's support.
- In the midst of the siege, on December 30, 1097, a strong earthquake struck. Then the very next day on January 1, 1098, there was a striking red sky in the north (likely a low-latitude sighting of the [aurora borealis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aurora)). These two things---happening in quick succession, at that---were also taken to be divine signs.
- When the crusaders had finally successfully taken the city only to themselves get besieged by the Muslim relieving army led by [Kerbogha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kerbogha) (a series of events we will go over in more detail [below]()), the trapped and starving crusaders viewed the blazing meteor streaking through the atmosphere on June 14, 1098 as favorable a sign from God, boosting morale ahead of an engagement with the army surrounding them.

One might go on.

{{% /note %}}

<!-- slide-break -->

Unfortunately, the fanaticism that took over here caused much greater damage than just the deaths of the brainwashed themselves. Put simply, the People's Crusade was a walking catastrophe, looting and pillaging wherever it went (including in the Christian territories they passed through). Even worse than that, some of the leaders like [Volkmar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volkmar_%28crusader%29) and [Emicho](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emicho_%28crusader%29) stirred up the mob to massacre Jews, in organized pogroms (cf. the [Rhineland Massacres](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhineland_massacres)).

The same sort of sentiment is thought to underpin some of the [horrific slaughter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Massacre_of_Jerusalem_(1099)) that followed the later capture of Jerusalem in 1099. Again, no more People's Crusade at this point, but that doesn't mean that all the ideas completely died with them.

I should note that how much of the violence and brutality was definitively driven by runaway apocalyptic sentiment is still something of a debate in the academy. The theory goes that the crusaders thought that both Jews and Muslims had to be destroyed or converted for the Earth to reach the end of days. The problem is, there are also other plausible explanations for the violence too. For example, the Medieval mindset commonly justified antisemitism (up to and including outright violence) by arguing that the Jews were the ones who crucified Jesus.

I should be clear that neither one of these things is any sort of excuse for slaughtering innocents. The point is not that the crusaders doing this was at all OK, but that figuring out *why* they did it is actually not as simple as one might think.

#### Summary points {#summary-points}

The formal armies of the First Crusade did not launch until the latter half of 1066. Once they arrived in the East, there arose the matter of how exactly the crusaders would interact with the administration of the Eastern Empire. For all his appreciation that the armies had come to his aid against the Seljuks, [Alexios I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexius_I_Komnenos) nonetheless had grave concerns about the potential threat the Western forces posed to Constantinople. After all, these were in essence foreign armies, not his own.

To protect his capital and secure his borders, Alexios I divided the incoming crusader forces and used the [Bosporus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bosporus) as a geographic barrier. The crusaders possessed no navy or transport ships of their own, so they were entirely dependent on the Byzantine imperial fleet to ferry them across the deep waters of the Bosporus.

Before ferrying the crusaders across, Alexios I forced them to swear an oath to him, guaranteeing that lands they conquered would be given back to the Byzantine Empire (rather than seized and kept for themselves, for example). By treating the boat crossing as a reward for submission, he successfully compelled the ambitious princes of Europe to sign away their future conquests before they ever set foot in Turkish territory.

As soon as a specific leader took the oath, Alexios I immediately shuttled that leader's troops across the strait. This tactic prevented the various Western armies from massing together into a singular force outside Constantinople's walls, which helped minimize the risk of an attack on the city.

<!-- slide-break -->

At any rate, by the time the crusader armies had arrived and been ferried across (after rather unenthusiastically agreeing to Alexios I's oath to hand over conquered lands), they set out to start recapturing territory. They first set their sights on [Nicea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicaea), and put it under siege.

Now sieges in medieval warfare tended to be much less exciting than in the movies. Rather than throwing soldiers at city walls (which usually only got them killed rather pointlessly, without much gain to show for it), the normal tactic was to try and surround and subsequently starve out the defenders. This often to led to somewhat dull stalemates until a lack of food forced one of the sides to negotiate terms. (I say sides plural, because oftentimes it was also no easy task keeping the besieging army supplied, like we will talk about more in a moment).

So, for example, part of the [Siege of Nicea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Nicaea) involved a Byzantine naval blockade of [Lake İznik](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_%C4%B0znik), which was being used to supply the defenders.

<!-- slide-break -->

In the end, it was not some grand assault on the walls that actually led to the city being taken, but a Turkish surrender to Byzantine general [Manuel Boutoumites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manuel_Boutoumites). Realizing they were defeated after the arrival of Byzantine lake-borne reinforcements, the Turkish garrison agreed to surrender to the Byzantine Empire rather than face slaughter and looting by the Latin crusaders. The surrender was actually staged, with a sort of play put on. Under Alexios I's orders, a different Byzantine general named [Tatikios](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tatikios) assaulted the walls of the city with the crusaders, while Boutoumites performed a simultaneous token attack to create the illusion that the imperial forces had breached the walls by force, although in reality this was all fake. In this way, the Turks surrendered to Boutoumites (the Byzantine forces), not the crusaders. 

After occupying the city, Boutoumites subsequently forbade the crusaders from looting or plundering the city for money and supplies, and in fact only let them enter the city in small groups (to prevent such things). This was done in order to preserve the city and its treasures for the Byzantine Empire, and keep them out of the hands of the Western crusaders.

The crusaders were greatly angered by this. Alexios I attempted to placate them with generous gifts of horses, money, and supplies, but the situation was nevertheless seen as a definite betrayal. To add insult to injury, Boutoumites would not permit the crusaders to leave until they had all sworn an oath of vassalage to Alexios I, if they had not yet done so in Constantinople.

<!-- slide-break -->

After Nicea, the armies marched on [Antioch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antioch). Antioch (modern [Antakya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antakya)) lay in a strategic location on the crusaders' route to Judea, through the [Syrian Coastal mountain range](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syrian_Coastal_Mountain_Range). Its strategic value for the crusaders was immense, as supplies, reinforcements and retreat could all be controlled by the city. 

Knowing all this, the crusaders decide to besiege the city (beginning in October 1097), even despite its formidable defenses. At this time, Antioch had high walls and strong fortifications, as well as a large amount of supplies stored within the city (partly as a result of the Seljuk governor anticipating the crusaders' siege in advance). All of these things together meant that the [Siege of Antioch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Antioch) was a very tough nut for the crusaders to crack. In fact, the siege dragged on for many months. After the first bit wherein they picked clean the surrounding lands, supplying the besieging army became a massive headache, and there was even widespread starvation. This led to some crusaders deserting.

<!-- slide-break -->

The Byzantine representative sent as an advisor to the crusaders (Tatikios---yes, the same one from the [Siege of Nicea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Nicaea)) departed the Siege of Antioch in February 1098. The reasons for his departure are contradictory in different sources (notably in the Byzantine accounts vs. crusader accounts), and in fact, his reasons for leaving are still debated in the academy to this day.

The Byzantine accounts say he left due to a fear of assassination:

- [Bohemond of Taranto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bohemond_I_of_Antioch), a powerful Norman leader, privately pulled Tatikios aside and warned him that the other crusader princes were plotting to murder him.
- Bohemond claimed the crusaders believed Emperor Alexios I was secretly collaborating with the Seljuk Turks, and withholding vital supplies.
- Modern historians generally agree Bohemond fabricated this threat to deliberately engineer Tatikios's departure. By driving the Emperor’s official representative away, Bohemond could later claim the Byzantines had abandoned the crusade, freeing him to break his oath and claim lordship of Antioch for himself.

The Latin/crusader accounts say he left due to cowardice and betrayal:

- Western accounts flatly label Tatikios a coward, an enemy, and a liar who fled when conditions grew too severe.
- By February 1098, the crusaders were enduring a brutal winter blockade, and were plagued by disease, starvation, and the impending arrival of a massive Muslim relief army led by [Kerbogha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kerbogha). Latin sources argue Tatikios simply abandoned a seemingly hopeless cause.

<!-- slide-break -->

A third perspective argues that Tatikios left to secure supplies:

- Tatikios publicly stated he was traveling to nearby Byzantine ports and Cyprus to organize and ship desperately needed food supplies, siege weaponry, and imperial reinforcements back to the starving army.
- In support of such a theory, on March 4, 1098 (a few weeks after the departure of Tatikios), a fleet bearing food supplies and siege materials for the crusader army did in fact arrive at the [port of St. Simeon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Port_Saint_Symeon).

{{% sidenote %}}

While this fleet is likely to have brought supplies originating from the Byzantine Empire, fascinatingly, medieval chroniclers describe the ships themselves as being an *English* fleet, supposedly associated with the exiled Anglo-Saxon claimant [Edgar Ætheling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edgar_%C3%86theling)), who fled England after the Norman conquest.

{{% /sidenote %}}

Whatever the case, the departure of Tatikios in early spring in some ways blinded Alexios I to the true situation at Antoich. This lack of reliable information about what was truly going on had important consequences later on, which we'll get to.

<!-- slide-break -->

Various things happened during the siege in the spring of 1098, but to make a long story short, the crusaders were put in a bind when the Muslim relief army headed by Kerbogha started bearing down on them. The crusaders concluded that they would have to take the city before Kerbogha arrived if they were to have any chance of survival. Now, by this time, Bohemond had secretly established contact with someone inside the city named [Firouz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Firouz). This man offered to let Bohemond into the city in exchange for money and a title.

Bohemond, in turn, approached the other crusaders and offered access to the city through Firouz if they would agree to make Bohemond the Prince of Antioch. Some of the other crusaders were furious and argued that the city should be handed over to Alexios I (as they had agreed when they left Constantinople in 1097), but ultimately many of the leaders, faced with a desperate situation, gave in to Bohemond's demand.

And so once again, the siege ended not with a grand storming of the walls, but with some form of inside help. By the end of the day on June 3, 1098 the crusaders controlled most of the city, and this proved to be just in time, for Kerbogha's relief army arrived on June 5.

Within just a few days, the roles had been reversed. Now the crusaders were defending the walls of Antioch, and Seljuks were the ones besieging. Ultimately, despite having taken the city before Kerbogha's army arrived, the crusaders nonetheless found themselves in a rather terrible situation, because they still lacked food.

<!-- slide-break -->

On Jun 10, [Peter Bartholomew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Bartholomew) (this a different Peter than Peter the Hermit) came forward claiming to have had visions of St. Andrew, who told him that the [Holy Lance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holy_Lance) (i.e., the spear that pierced Jesus's side) was inside the city. While we may be skeptical (and in fact, some of the other crusaders were as well), there is no question that the "discovery of this relic" (which happened on June 15, allegedly) increased the morale of the desperate crusaders. On top of this, there was the aforementioned blazing meteor streaking through the atmosphere on June 14, which the crusaders also took as a positive sign from God.

Regardless of the truth of any of this, one cannot underestimate how important the frame of mind of soldiers is upon the battlefield. Many a superior force throughout history has lost (sometimes spectacularly) due to fear or lack of confidence about a battle, so regardless of the truth of these things, the fanatic zeal of the crusader army here likely played no small role in their battle against the surrounding Turkish army, a battle that came some two weeks later on June 28. This battle was brief and disastrous for the Seljuks (partly because they were not terribly united to begin with, and many ended up deserting Kerbogha).

<!-- slide-break -->

Several things happened in the aftermath of the crusaders breaking out of the Muslim siege. Bohemond claimed Antioch as his own, conveniently claiming Alexios I had deserted the crusade, and thus invalidated all of their oaths to him. (Cf. again the theory that he intentionally engineered Tatikios's departure). In a stroke of luck for Bohemond's propaganda, Alexios I actually did abandon the crusaders in some fashion, although not so much intentionally, but because of the information vacuum we mentioned before. In June 1098, Alexios I was actually leading forces to assist the crusaders when he came across [Stephen-Henry of Blois](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stephen-Henry_of_Blois) and other crusaders who had deserted. Stephen is thought to have left Antioch on June 2, whereas the crusaders captured the city on June 3. That single day made all the difference, because the story Stephen brought to Alexios I was one of doom and gloom: he had seen Kerbogha's massive army encamped near Antioch, and assumed all hope was lost. He managed to convince Alexios I that the crusaders were as good as dead, and Alexios I acted on this report by turning his army around and heading back to Constantinople. As you can imagine, when the crusaders somewhat miraculously broke out of Kerbogha's siege all on their own, the lack of imperial support was very much noted, giving rise to even more resentment.

##### Should we as Christians care about so-called holy relics? No, we should not

{{< properties

stags=""

>}}

The [Holy Lance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holy_Lance) (i.e., the spear that pierced Jesus's side) made an appearance here in the Siege of Antioch. This is far from the only Christian relic to grace the pages of history. For example, some other famous ones:

- The [Shroud of Turin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shroud_of_Turin) (i.e., the burial cloth used to wrap Jesus)
- Fragments of the [True Cross](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/True_Cross) (i.e., the cross upon which Jesus was crucified)
- The [Holy Chalice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holy_Chalice) (i.e., the vessel that Jesus used at the Last Supper)
- The [Crown of Thorns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crown_of_thorns) (i.e., the crown of thorns that Jesus wore during the crucifixion)
- Etc.

Catholics, venerate these things. So what to make of this?

<!-- slide-break -->

Put bluntly, all of it is complete nonsense. It is extremely doubtful whether any of these relics are even authentic, but *even if they were*, so what? Nothing in scripture gives us any indication that any of these things were specially set apart to be treated as holy and sacred.

By way of contrast, the relics the Jews were supposed to treat as sacred---like the [Ark of the Covenant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ark_of_the_Covenant), for example---were clearly specified in scripture. So if God wants us humans to treat some physical object as special and important, He will tell us directly. That is the pattern of Old Testament practice, at any rate.

But we are now under the New Covenant; the shadows and symbols of the Law have now passed away. Just like physical pilgrimages miss the point of our true spiritual pilgrimage to Zion, if what you get out of the biblical sacrament of communion (for example) is an obsession with the specific cup Jesus used, you sort of missed the point. The Body and Blood of Christ are metaphors for who He is and what He did for us on the cross. What has that to do with physical objects? Nothing. Nothing at all.

So once more, we should keep our eyes firmly fixed upon the spiritual, rather than becoming preoccupied with the physical.

#### Summary points {#summary-points}

After the crusaders managed to break through Kerbogha's encirclement (thereby cementing a Seljuk defeat at Antioch), many of Alexios I's initial goals for the crusade had been met. The Seljuks had been pushed back in Asia Minor, and Constantinople itself was no longer under threat.

However, just because he may perhaps have been satisfied with this as a stopping point did not mean everyone else felt the same way. As we've already discussed, whether Pope Urban II preached Jerusalem from the beginning or lay preachers sort of pivoted public sentiment that direction in a more grassroots sort of way, either way, in the eyes of many of the Western crusaders, Jerusalem came to be viewed as the ultimate prize. And let's not forget the simmering apocalyptic/Millenarian sentiment we mentioned previously either. Some crusaders were true believers in the notion that they were on a mission to usher in the reign of Christ and the end of the world. How then could they give up half way?

There was also the rupture in relations that happened when Alexios I failed to come to the crusaders' aid at Antioch due to turning back after hearing false reports of the crusaders' defeat. Thereafter, Bohemond of Taranto was all to happy to use this to bolster his accusations of Byzantine abandonment, since it gave him a pretext to seize Antioch for himself. In turn, Alexios I took this as the Latin lords breaking the oaths sworn to him, which were very clear that Byzantine lands taken back from the Seljuks were to be *returned*. He was thus not terribly happy either. (Of course, he especially did not like Bohemond, since Bohemond and his father ([Robert Guiscard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Guiscard)) had spent some years prior to the crusades [invading Byzantine lands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bohemond_I_of_Antioch#Byzantine_wars)).

So it was that when some of the crusading forces wanted to continue on to Jerusalem, they did so without the accompaniment of imperial troops.

<!-- slide-break -->

{{% note %}}

This is *not* to say that the crusaders received no aid from Alexios I and the Byzantine Empire on their Jerusalem campaign at the end of the First Crusade. For example:

- **Naval supplies**: During the march south, the crusaders stuck close to the Mediterranean coast precisely because they relied on maritime resupply. Without these active Byzantine maritime supply lines, the crusaders would have run out of critical supplies long before reaching Palestine.
- **Diplomatic cover**: Alexios I used Byzantine diplomatic networks to ensure local governors and markets along the coast allowed the crusaders to purchase food, horses, and so on. In this way--that is, by maintaining a general framework of imperial protection, even after military alliances soured---Alexios I prevented the march from turning into a total logistical disaster.

And let's not forget all the Byzantine Empire had already done for the crusaders by this point (such as providing local guides, elite troops under General Tatikios, and heavy funding through all the military campaigns in Anatolia). Put simply, all this imperial support is what allowed the crusade to survive long enough to attempt the march on Jerusalem in the first place.

Why am I harping on all this? Because the Western crusader propaganda of the time villainized Alexios I and his supposed lack of further assistance. It is really rather ironic, if you think about it, but there you have it.

How to view Byzantium's role also comes up as a topic in academic discourse about the crusades, as we already mentioned when quoting [The Byzantine Background to the First Crusade](https://deremilitari.org/2013/06/the-byzantine-background-to-the-first-crusade/) above, and the somewhat contrasting views of older scholars like Steven Runciman vs. newer scholars like Jonathan Riley-Smith and others. I again won't take much of a side here myself, aside from noting that the more recent views are much more dominant amongst most current historians, as best I can gather.

{{% /note %}}

<!-- slide-break -->

Now, the Byzantine Empire (and, frankly, Christendom as a whole) had at this point not held Jerusalem for some 400 years (cf. the [First Muslim conquest of Jerusalem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Muslim_conquest_of_Jerusalem) in the 600s). Rather, Jerusalem had been part of Muslim empires for all these centuries. But now, stoked by the propaganda, the crusaders believed that they could take Jerusalem. Even more than that, they believed that they were *called* to take Jerusalem.

And so... they did. In 1099 the crusaders assaulted Jerusalem (cf. the [Siege of Jerusalem (1099)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Jerusalem_%281099%29)), and after taking it, massacred many Muslims and Jews already living there (cf. the [Massacre of Jerusalem (1099)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Massacre_of_Jerusalem_%281099%29)), echoing the brutality exhibited previously in the slaughter of Jews in the [Rhineland Massacres](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhineland_massacres). There is no defending this stuff, but happen it did.

In the mind of crusaders, this was not seen as conquest = taking lands that were not theirs. Remember that the Christian world (both the Byzantine world in the East and the Christian Western Empire) viewed themselves as extensions of the Roman Empire. That is important because in the past Rome had firmly held Judea for many centuries. In this way, conquering Jerusalem was seen more as "freeing" these lands and restoring them to their rightful owners, rather than taking them by force. We might scoff at this logic a bit in the modern day (rather convenient in terms of *jus ad bellum*, isn't it?), but that was the logic.

This conquest of Jerusalem in July 1099 is often seen as the end of the First Crusade (although more formally speaking, the [Battle of Ascalon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Ascalon) in the next month was truly the last military clash).

#### Summary points {#summary-points}

As we previously mentioned, the very notion of what a crusade even was and why it was important (what we might term the *crusading spirit*) was sort of worked out on the fly as the First Crusade played out. However, after this first crusade had run its course and a "crusading framework" existed (if you want to look at things that way), additional crusades followed.

Part of this crusading framework that was established were certain crusade-oriented groups: the [Knights Templar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knights_Templar) and [Knights Hospitaller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knights_Hospitaller), among others. These organizations started out with somewhat different purposes, initially:

- **Knights Templar**: Founded around 1118 in Jerusalem, their sole initial and permanent focus was military protection---guarding the roads and routes used by vulnerable pilgrims visiting holy sites. The Templars got their name because they used as their headquarters a mosque that at the time was housed on the top of the [Temple Mount](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temple_Mount).
- **Knights Hospitaller**: In the mid 11th century, merchants from [Duchy of Amalfi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duchy_of_Amalfi) founded a hospital in Jerusalem dedicated to John the Baptist. The hospital was staffed by [Benedictine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order_of_Saint_Benedict) monks, who cared for sick, poor, or injured Christian pilgrims to the Holy Land. After the conquest of Jerusalem in 1099 during the First Crusade, the Hospitallers rose in prominence and were recognized as a distinct monastic order by [Pope Paschal II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pope_Paschal_II) in 1113. The Hospitallers got their name from this hospital they ran in Jerusalem.

<!-- slide-break -->

It might be tempting to neatly categorize these monastic orders as military protectors and healers (respectively), however, the Hospitallers militarized in the 1120s and 1130s, hiring knights to defend their hospitals and estates. After that point, they too became a sort of military religious order, not so dissimilar from the Templars in practice.

Now, both of these groups arose to support and protect those coming down on pilgrimage to the Holy Land. Put simply, this protection was needed, because pilgrims faced danger not only from the Turks, but also raiders and bandits and opportunistic armies all between Europe and the Holy Land. On balance, it is really not so odd that paramilitary organizations popped up to fill this need for protection for pilgrims. What *was* somewhat odd is that these paramilitary organizations were run by monks. Given that traditional Christian monasticism strictly forbade the shedding of blood or bearing arms, these crusade-adjacent paramilitary monastic orders were therefore very much "something new".

During the height of the crusades in the 12th and 13th centuries, the Knights Hospitaller and the Knights Templar were roughly equal rivals in military power, wealth, and land holdings in the Holy Land. Both maintained active core forces of several hundred elite knights supplemented by thousands of sergeants, mercenaries, and vast European property networks. There were also the [Teutonic Knights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teutonic_Order) too, who Dr. Reeves did not mention. They were another important military religious order with ties to the crusades.

<!-- slide-break -->

In pop culture though, the Knights Templar are way more famous than all the rest, and it is somewhat interesting to hypothesize as to why. Some of the reasons Dr. Reeves went over as to the fame of the Templars seem to me to apply equally to all of these military religious orders:

- All of these military religious orders had warrior monks, which were a very novel concept at the time.
- All of these military religious orders were "far off" and exotic, from the perspective of Europe. More vanilla monastic orders were much less mysterious, since they were just down the road. Not these orders, though. This was made all the more impactful by the fact that it was not just any distant land where these orders were operating, but the Holy Land itself.
- All of these military religious orders amassed wealth and land and other things within Europe itself. For example, people would leave land, money, and even entire estates to the orders in their wills. Using this wealth, both orders built churches and castles and so on in Europe, not just in the Levant.
- All of these military religious orders exercised their money and power mostly independent of the feudal system and other conventional forms of societal organization. This made them seem proportionally more foreign.

So given that these factors are common to all these orders, again, why is it the Templars in particular that have such an astounding reputation?

<!-- slide-break -->

One argument is simply that the Templars stopped existing, and the others never did. Conspiracy theories don't go wild about groups that are still active, because the still active groups shut them down. But conspiracy theories can freely abound when it comes to dead groups; nobody will squash them or correct them.

Of course, the end of the Templars was also not some boring slow decline either, but just about as sensational as things could possibly get, and that too contributed to the mystery and intrigue surrounding them. You can read a summary of the destruction of the Templars [here](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knights_Templar#Arrests,_charges_and_dissolution), or a more complete account [here](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trials_of_the_Knights_Templar). The short version is that the Templars were exterminated by [Pope Clement V](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pope_Clement_V) and [King Philip IV of France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philip_IV_of_France), with Philip IV pushing for the order's destruction mostly because he was deeply in debt to them, and wanted to get rid of them in general, because their organizational structure (which spanned across multiple nations) was very hard for monarchs like him to control.

In order to legitimize the blatant persecution of what amounted to his political opponents, Philip IV had them tried on trumped up [charges of heresy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trials_of_the_Knights_Templar#Charges_against_the_Templars), some of which (e.g., idolatry, institutional sodomy, etc.) were exaggerated so outrageously and salaciously that of course ever after the Templars became cloaked in an air of secret impropriety.

And it wasn't just trials, but also public executions, even burning Templars at the stake (including the last [Templar grand master](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_grand_masters_of_the_Knights_Templar), [Jacques de Molay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacques_de_Molay)).

Even though almost no modern scholars think any of these charges were true in even small part, that did not stop popular imagination from filling in the blanks in the sense of "no smoke without fire". And *this* is why the Templars have always loomed exceptionally large in pop culture, while the other military religious orders did not. At least, that is my take on the matter.

<!-- slide-break -->

At any rate, these military religious orders fit into the West's continuing need for force projection in the East. After the First Crusade, all the lands that had been conquered had to be defended from without, and governed from within---all of which took a significant amount of capital, both human and financial. One must not forget that these lands had been taken by force, and many of the people in these lands were not Christian (or if they were, they were Byzantine, and therefore had different practices than the Western Church, and were not always meekly subservient).

In fact, the crusaders were not always terribly successful in holding on to what they conquered. The Turks took back some lands, for example, which in turn led to additional PR campaigns in the West to rally support to go reconquer the lands for Christendom once again. This never-ending quagmire likely would never have come about had the crusaders stopped with reconquering Anatolia for the Byzantines, but since they did not---and instead chose to go and conquer additional lands in hostile territory---the following crusades became almost inevitable. The West exerted enormous amounts of energy and time and money and resources to hold onto its lands in the Levant, and this cycle of conquering lands, losing them, reconquering them, and so on led to a sort of standardization and normalization of the crusading spirit in Europe. This was one important legacy of the First Crusade

Perhaps the other most significant legacy of the First Crusade was another crack in relations between Eastern Christendom and and Western Christendom. You would think that banding together against a common Muslim enemy might provide an excellent opportunity for long-term reconciliation, but in fact both sides ended up rather dissatisfied with the other. The Western crusaders because they thought the Eastern Empire had abandoned them, and the Eastern Empire because they thought the Western crusaders failed to hold to their oaths of giving conquered lands back to Byzantium. Even if the conflict had not yet boiled over into outright hostilities, the First Crusade further increased the divide that the Great Schism of 1054 had rendered official.

#### Follow-on topics

##### What if instead of the Crusades people had turned all their zeal towards spiritual growth?

I want us to imagine for a moment that the enormous resource investment we are talking about here in the Crusades had instead gone towards spiritual growth for all the common people in Europe. How much better off would humanity of have been!

In fact, if you look throughout history at the amount of resources sunk into funding spiritual growth in any significant capacity, you will almost always find that it is puny compared to everything else people spend money on in the name of Christ. To be clear, this is as much a demand problem as it is a supply problem. That is to say, when the extreme events of 1095 went down, the common people were willing to be convinced that it was the end of the world and march off to conquer Jerusalem, but not so willing to live quiet lives of discipline and faithfulness, keeping their heads down and minding their own business while trying to follow God as best as they could. Where is the glory in that? The deliverance from life's trials?

The glory and deliverance is spiritual, is the problem. And that won't cut it when what people want is physical solutions. And so we get the Crusades, and other things like them.

<!-- slide-break -->

In fact, things have not changed, even to this day. Just look at the priorities of American Christians in the 21st century. What if all the money and energy and resources Christians spent trying to win physical victories in the political sphere and the culture war was instead spent funding serious Bible study and spiritual growth? But no. People would still rather focus on the physical. And so we quote Ecclesiastes:

{{% scripture p="Ecclesiastes 1:9 | NIV11" %}}

What has been will be again, what has been done will be done again; there is nothing new under the sun.
