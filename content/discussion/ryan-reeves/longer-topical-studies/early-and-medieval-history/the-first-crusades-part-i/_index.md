#### Summary points {#summary-points}

This lesson is the first of two parts going over the Crusades, the Christian pilgrimage-holy-wars to Jerusalem and surrounding regions of the Middle East.

Few periods in history have the same level of popular investment as the Crusades, nor the same level of narrative-shaping impact (in terms of affecting how people think about broad trends or patterns in history and sociology). This has been ever more the case post-[9/11](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/September_11_attacks).

<!-- slide-break -->

In our modern times characterized by a renewed focus on religiously-motivated violence, the Crusades are something that is discussed frequently---not only at the level of everyday people, but also at the level of politics:

- For example, in 2001, not long after 9/11, speaking at [Georgetown](https://www.georgetown.edu/), [Bill Clinton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bill_Clinton) placed the blame for faith-based violence on religious extremism---and not just that within [Islam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islam). He said the religious extremism of the Middle Ages had caused the Crusades and all the atrocities therein. (Note the strong language of moral condemnation; "atrocities" is far from a neutral label).
- [George W. Bush](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_W._Bush) also used language and imagery related to what happened in the Crusades when speaking about the [Iraq War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iraq_War)... or at least the ongoing popular understanding of what happened in the Crusades.
- More recently, [Barack Obama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barack_Obama), speaking at a prayer breakfast, urged those who identify as Christians to remain humble because we too have atrocities in our background.

The amount of conversation and debate that erupted on account of such statements was really quite striking. Clearly, people have ideas and opinions about what happened during the Crusades, and what we are to make of them. Anyone who has ever had to teach on the subject knows that this is one area of history that tends to spark passionate feelings, and that can make it decidedly difficult to talk about objectively. For example, many Protestants have a very negative view of the Crusades, and view them largely as the result of problematic Catholic doctrine and corruption within the Church hierarchy.

<!-- slide-break -->

While there are an exceedingly large number of things one would need to discuss to make a full analysis of the cause and effect of the Crusades (rather than only touching the tip of the iceberg, so to speak), we don't have the space here to do that sort of deep dive. Instead, we will predominantly be laying out evidence against a single point of view that we will argue is too simplistic: that the Crusades were a completely unprovoked attack on peaceable Muslims by bloodthirsty Christians who wanted land and loot. This is the sort of position typically put forward by those who wish to cast the entire affair in an overwhelmingly negative light, and make some point to the effect that Christians too have innocent blood on their hands. (At least if one buys that it is ever proper to blame the majority for the sins of the minority, which I do not).

Now, we aren't going to be arguing that there were absolutely no selfish motives in play here (to be frank, when is that *ever* true in history?), but merely discuss some historical context that sort of explodes the completely neat, tidy version of this particular narrative. In other words, we won't be pretending there is nothing to criticize on the Christian side, but we also won't be overly whitewashing the Muslim side (like is necessary in order to paint the Crusades in the one-sided overwhelmingly negative way some people do).

As in most other conflicts in history, when viewed from a neutral perspective, you can make a good case that nobody was completely innocent, and both sides had plenty of problems. That is the approach I take in analyzing the Crusades, at any rate.

<!-- slide-break -->

In the video, Dr. Reeves says this:

{{% quote work="The First Crusades (Part I)" workurl="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7bGxMcSHOmI" author="Ryan Reeves" authorurl="https://www.youtube.com/@RyanReevesM" %}}

The pope did not call The Crusade in an effort to conquer land; there was no effort in the West initially to move armies/vast numbers of troops at enormous expense in an effort to retake the Holy Land for the Western Church or for the papacy. When the pope called for the Crusade, he really only wanted an army of a few thousand to travel down there and to assist the Byzantine empire in their ongoing war and struggle to define the borders of their lands over against the [Seljuk Turks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seljuk_Empire).

So what's going on here is not a land grab or a money grab; it's not necessarily corrupt people initially saying that they are going to enact a war in an effort to steal money or land or any of these kinds of things, *per se*.

In fact the initial pact (the initial Crusader oath) required that whatever lands were conquered---if they had been formerly Byzantine lands---that they would be handed back to the Byzantine emperor immediately.

Secondly and most importantly (this is the one that comes up more often than not)---the Crusades were *not* the first [sally](https://www.vocabulary.com/dictionary/sally%20forth) of the war or the struggle between the Middle East and the Western World.

{{% /quote %}}

<!-- slide-break -->

There are several points made here, as it seems to me:

1. That retaking the Holy Land was, at the least, not the only initial motivation for the Crusades. For example, there was also the matter of aiding Byzantine Christians in their conflict with the Muslim Seljuk Turks.
3. That the fact that Crusader oath made knights yield conquered lands over to the Byzantine Empire shows that personal greed could not have played a role as much as some people say.
4. That this is not the first conflict between Christians and Muslims, and so must be analyzed in terms of all that came before.

I should note that how much of the First Crusade was motivated by coming to the aid of the Byzantine Empire (i.e., *defending* territorial borders) and how much by a desire to reconquer the Holy Land (i.e., *expanding* territorial borders) is actually rather controversial, as far as scholarly debates go. In [The Byzantine Background to the First Crusade](https://deremilitari.org/2013/06/the-byzantine-background-to-the-first-crusade/) (an excellent source for background on the Byzantine side of things), Paul Magadalino days this:

{{% quote work="The Byzantine Background to the First Crusade" workurl="https://deremilitari.org/2013/06/the-byzantine-background-to-the-first-crusade/" author="Paul Magadalino" authorurl="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Magdalino" %}}

There seems to be general agreement that Urban II would not have preached the crusade if Alexios I had not asked for help against the Turks.

{{% /quote %}}

<!-- slide-break -->

Unpacking this statement requires us to have a bit of background:

- [Byzantine emperor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_emperor) [Alexius I Comnenus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexius_I_Comnenus) sent envoys to the [Council of Piacenza](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Council_of_Piacenza) in March 1095, requesting military assistance against the [Seljuk Turks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seljuk_Turks), who were encroaching upon Byzantine lands.
- In November 1095, there was a different council called the [Council of Clermont](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Council_of_Clermont) called by [Pope Urban II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pope_Urban_II). The actual purpose of this council was actually pretty unrelated to the Crusades, but on the last day (November 27, 1095) Pope Urban II gave a famous speech that served as the call to arms that kicked off the First Crusade. This is viewed by most scholars as being in response to Alexius I's prior request for aid.

<!-- slide-break -->

As is ever the case in historical analysis, the sources we have that describe Pope Urban II's speech at Clermont do not all agree with each other. Excluding a letter about the council that Pope Urban II wrote himself, we have five main sources of information:

- The anonymous [*Gesta Francorum*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gesta_Francorum) ("The Deeds of the Franks" dated c. 1100/1101)
- [Fulcher of Chartres](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fulcher_of_Chartres), who was present at the council, in his [*Gesta Francorum Iherusalem peregrinantium*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gesta_Francorum_Iherusalem_peregrinantium) (c. 1100–1105)
- [Robert the Monk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_the_Monk), who may have been present at the council, in Historia Hierosolymitana (c. 1107)
- [Baldric, archbishop of Dol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baldric,_archbishop_of_Dol) (c. 1105)
- [Guibert de Nogent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guibert_de_Nogent), [*Dei gesta per Francos*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dei_gesta_per_Francos) (c. 1107/1108)

<!-- slide-break -->

According to [Dana Carleton Munro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dana_Carleton_Munro)'s [*The speech of Pope Urban II at Clermont*](https://catalog.hathitrust.org/Record/011212737/Home), all versions except the *Gesta Francorum* agree that Pope Urban II talked about:

1. The violence of European society and the necessity of maintaining the Peace of God
2. Helping the Greeks, who had asked for assistance
3. The crimes being committed against Christians in the East
4. A new kind of war, an armed pilgrimage, and of rewards in heaven, where remission of sins was offered to any who might die in the undertaking

Past these goals, Robert the Monk's account of Clermont also specifically mentions the reconquest of the Holy Land (i.e., on top of merely aiding the Greeks in their pre-existing conflict), which leads us to ask whether or not Pope Urban II actually spoke of the reconquest of Jerusalem right from the get-go. Not all the sources specifically mention Jerusalem as the ultimate goal, so it is hard to say with certainty. It doesn't help that all of these versions were written *after* Jerusalem had been captured in 1099, and it is therefore difficult to know what was actually said versus what was recreated in the aftermath of the successful crusade in a form of post-hoc retcon. Either way, it is clear that no matter how things started, the First Crusade clearly ended up targeting Jerusalem in fairly short order.

<!-- slide-break -->

Some scholars think that the papal goalposts were adjusted on a rolling basis based upon the growing popularity of the Crusade among the common people. That is, after the papacy saw the growing tide of popular support and realized they might be able to accomplish more than initially thought, they dynamically updated the public-facing messaging. This point of view is somewhat persuasive, insofar as the papacy seems to have wanted to recruit knights initially, but instead in practice ended up recruiting a bunch of peasants. For example, compare [Peter the Hermit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_the_Hermit) and the so-called ["People's Crusade"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Crusade#Peter_the_Hermit_and_the_People's_Crusade). Whatever the papacy's initial intentions, this popular movement clearly wanted to target Jerusalem specifically. So it is possible that the papacy decided to pivot to use this groundswell of popular support, rather than planning for it from the beginning. Although you might also argue that the papacy always intended to spark and then harness a wave of popular support, which is why Pope Urban II promised that anyone who participated would receive immediate remission of sins. That is quite the carrot to dangle, especially in context, given how most viewed forgiveness of sin at that point in time.

<!-- slide-break -->

So which is it---did the papacy pivot to go along with popular support, or intentionally manipulate public sentiment behind-the-scenes in order to bring about the outcome they planned beforehand? We needn't necessarily take a firm position here to accomplish our goal of arguing that the motivations behind the Crusades are objectively much less clear-cut than popular narratives often suggest (including the specific narrative that the Crusades were an act of unprovoked aggression on the part of greedy Christians who wanted land and loot). Rather than going down this path of trying to boil the Crusades down to neat categories of black and white that can serve to provoke emotional outrage (either for or against), in my opinion, it is much healthier as an academic approach to try to take a step back and come up with evidence-based explanatory models that make a good-faith effort to account for *all* data (not just cherry-picked data supporting only one side). This is much, much harder to do properly, and will necessarily involve speaking mostly in terms of probabilities and hypotheses (rather than moral absolutes). It also has the disadvantage of getting dry and technical rather quickly. But this path is the only one likely to avoid gross oversimplification.

<!-- slide-break -->

So what then will we argue sparked the First Crusade, if it was not merely a blatant land grab? Well, I think you go too far the other way if you try to argue that the First Crusade was limited in scope to just being a defensive war to support the Byzantine Empire's borders against the encroaching Seljuk Turks. Those circumstances are certainly related to the launching of the First Crusade (and we shouldn't try to pretend otherwise), but I don't think it is appropriate to put 100% of the focus there either, as if that was all that was going on here.

Rather, the reasons for the First Crusade seem to be decidedly complex and multifaceted. In my opinion, [this Reddit post](https://www.reddit.com/r/AskHistorians/comments/1k3m8un/comment/mo3veam/) on [r/AskHistorians](https://www.reddit.com/r/AskHistorians/) does a good job explaining some of the [*casus belli*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Casus_belli), without weighting any one factor higher than is appropriate:

<!-- slide-break -->

{{% quote work="Is there any merit in the theory that the First Crusade started because Muslims were oppressing Christians in the Levant and preventing pilgrimages to Jerusalem?" workurl="https://www.reddit.com/r/AskHistorians/comments/1k3m8un/comment/mo3veam/" author="jogarz" authorurl="https://www.reddit.com/user/jogarz/" %}}

In short, yes, there is merit in stating that the First Crusade was in part a response to Muslim violence against Christians. However, in part is a key qualifier here. There were several motivating factors for the Crusade. To briefly describe some of the other motivations:

- A desire to liberate the physical holy places, distinct from their Christian inhabitants. Jerusalem looked large in the thoughts of Western Christians at the time, and the idea of the [Church of the Holy Sepulcher](https://www.reddit.com/search/?q=Church+of+the+Holy+Sepulcher+First+Crusade&cId=074a2485-f01b-4d78-b7cf-ea9704060641&iId=a64676be-441c-4aa3-8bc4-74f806a4f05a) languishing in Muslim hands was seen as a humiliation and insult to Christians.
- A desire to reunite the Western and Eastern churches. The Papacy hoped that by assisting the Byzantines against their enemies, the Great Schism could eventually be healed and the universal church could be reunified.
<!-- slide-break -->
- A desire to strengthen Papal authority in Western Europe. The First Crusade took place at the height of the [Gregorian Reform movement](https://www.reddit.com/search/?q=Gregorian+Reform+movement+Papal+authority&cId=79f3c689-c4f5-4aca-8bd9-b49c64b72664&iId=f0b7d1c3-e735-4acc-9072-a69913a49dfc), which sought to purify corruption in the Church, in part by limiting secular influence over its affairs. This meant strengthening the power of the Papacy. By calling for and organizing such a momentous endeavor as the Crusade, the Popes could further their image as the paramount leaders of Western Christendom.
- A desire to provide an outlet for the violence endemic in Western Europe at the time. Ironically, the Crusades were directly connected to the 10th and 11th century European peace movement, which sought to limit the petty wars waged by the nobility and their knights. A common theme in Crusade preaching was that warriors should stop their sinful, selfish violence against their fellow Christians at home, and instead direct their energies towards a righteous war beyond the sea.

No single factor has complete explanatory power, and all played a role in creating the conditions that led to the First Crusade. So while it is valid to state that violence and oppression towards Christians motivated the First Crusade, it is important to remember that this wasn’t the *only* motivation for the Crusades.

{{% /quote %}}

<!-- slide-break -->

It would also be remiss of me not to mention here that as I understand it, there is actually a good bit of disagreement in the academy about how to characterize the Crusades, with some modern scholarly takes essentially breaking completely with past views on the subject. To quote [The Byzantine Background to the First Crusade](https://deremilitari.org/2013/06/the-byzantine-background-to-the-first-crusade/) again:

{{% quote work="The Byzantine Background to the First Crusade" workurl="https://deremilitari.org/2013/06/the-byzantine-background-to-the-first-crusade/" author="Paul Magadalino" authorurl="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Magdalino" %}}

Fortunately for those of us who are not historians of the crusades, there is no lack of up-to-date literature to help us concentrate our minds. Indeed, the last thirty-five years have seen a remarkable flowering of crusade scholarship in North America, in Israel, in Germany, and above all in Britain, where a generation of productive and dedicated scholars, mainly under the leadership of Jonathan Riley-Smith, is rewriting the whole history of the crusading movement, in practice and in theory, from beginning to end. They have already published significant contributions to the re-interpretation of the First Crusade – I mention Riley-Smith’s own book on the subject, published in 1986, John France’s military history of the crusade, and Marcus Bull’s study of lay piety in south western France in the eleventh century – and we can no doubt look forward to the appearance of more volumes occasioned by the ninth centenary celebrations. The day is hopefully not far off when we will know all that can be known, or at least will have heard all that can usefully be said, about what was in Pope Urban’s mind when he preached at Clermont, and what was in the minds of the laymen who answered, or did not answer, his call to arms. What is certain is that the labours of so many energetic *milites Christi* make it unnecessary, and indeed impertinent, for a Byzantinist to pronounce on such matters. We have come a long way since the 1950s, when a Byzantinist, Sir Steven Runciman, could write an authoritative, best-selling history of the crusades.

<!-- slide-break -->

And yet, in moving on from Runciman, we have lost as well as gained. We have lost not only the combination of scholarly erudition and narrative ease which still make Runciman an unbeatable first introduction to the subject; we have also lost the sense of Byzantium as something integral to the crusading movement. Whereas Runciman stressed the role of the Byzantine emperor Alexios I (1081-1118) in initiating the crusade, directing the crusaders and helping them on their way, the two recent monographs by Jonathan Riley-Smith and John France have cast him as a much more marginal player. Riley-Smith suggests that relations with Byzantium were not uppermost in Urban II’s calculations, and France, after dismissing the evidence of the main Byzantine source, the biography of Alexios I by his daughter Anna Comnena, argues that Alexios did little to help the crusade and was indifferent to its fate once he had used it to recapture Nicaea. Neither of these judgements is highly momentous in itself, but taken together and in the context of the general drift of modern crusade scholarship, they can be regarded as symptomatic of a tendency to write Byzantium out of the script, or, to choose a metaphor appropriate to our small academic world of conferences and symposia, not to invite Byzantium to the party. Byzantium is barely mentioned in the *Oxford History of the Crusades*, edited by Jonathan Riley-Smith and published in 1995. Byzantium thus comes to be seen as an unwilling, passive and even obstructive channel that the crusade had to pass through in order to get from Western Europe to the Holy Land: the place that happened to be on the way, or in the way. To use another metaphor, Byzantium was the temporary conductor of an electrical charge which the empire played no part in creating and had no interest in retaining. The implications of the fact that it received the current because its own battery needed recharging are not even considered.

{{% /quote %}}

<!-- slide-break -->

In other words, the way in which certain more recent scholars like [Jonathan Riley-Smith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jonathan_Riley-Smith) have approached the Crusades is very different from some older sources like [Steven Runciman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steven_Runciman). I am not here taking a position on which view to hold, so much as noting that there are in fact views plural to pick between, rather than one dominant paradigm that is universally held.

While I certainly cannot speak for him, parts of what Dr. Reeves says in the video seem to lean towards some of Riley-Smith's views, which hold that the Crusades were not really driven by a desire for material gain, *pace* popular narratives. Compare again Dr. Reeves's statement that I previously quoted: "[W]hat's going on here is not a land grab or a money grab; it's not necessarily corrupt people initially saying that they are going to enact a war in an effort to steal money or land or any of these kinds of things."

<!-- slide-break -->

In fact, you cannot discuss the Crusades in a vacuum, ignoring the hundreds of years of prior conflicts between Christians and Muslims (including many with the Muslim side being the aggressors, to be clear). Put simply, trying to blame the Crusades for all Islamic hostility towards Christianity is misguided at best, and outright propagandistic at worst. This notion that there were all these peaceful Muslims out there minding there own business until bloodthirsty Christians showed up and burned everything down (and only then did Muslims start fighting Christians, making the origin of the religious conflict between these two groups rest solely upon the shoulders of Christians) is plain fiction.

Ever since its conception, it was not uncommon for Islam to expand more through the sword than the pen, particularly when conquering the lands of those with markedly different cultures (like, say, Christians). Was it always bloody wars of conquest? Of course not. But it certainly was sometimes. Regardless of where exactly one draws the lines in categorizing specific events in the expansion of Islam, the point is that by the time you get to the Crusades, there was in fact plenty of Christian blood on Muslim hands. This does not necessarily justify what came after (since vengeance is to be the Lord's; cf. Romans 12:19). But it certainly *does* mean that the narrative articulated above about absolutely peaceful Muslims and completely bloodthirsty Christians is mostly nonsense.

<!-- slide-break -->

Rather, the Crusades merely mark the latest in a series of ongoing struggles between Christendom and Islam. Out West, there was the Muslim conquest of Spain, and their attempted expansion northwards and eastwards into parts of southern France (cf. the [Battle of Tours](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Tours)). Out East, the Byzantine Christians had been having land disputes with various Muslim political structures for many centuries. This is not to say that the two sides were never able to peacefully coexist (since there was plenty of that too in history); the point is, the Crusades were far from the first time the two religions had crossed blades, so to speak.

And in fact, the Crusades were far from the last time too. Frankly, all the way up until as late as the 16th century, European forces and Byzantine forces clashed with various Muslim states almost constantly.

So while the Crusades are indeed an enormously important part of the history of Christian-Muslim relations, they are not the only part of the story.

<!-- slide-break -->

#### Follow-on topics {#follow-on-topics}

##### Should we as Christians care about specific places, like Jerusalem? No, we should not

Whether or not the papacy initially intended for the Crusades to reconquer Jerusalem, that goal quickly got adopted in practice. It is no straw man to say that the Christian propaganda of the time did clearly paint the military expeditions as a form of holy war to once again take control of the holy land. (To be fair, historical Muslims also frequently justified wars using religion, although we cannot be overly anachronistic in this, since many singularly militaristic views on [jihad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jihad) are in fact quite modern).

So, to put it bluntly, is there anything valid about this? That is, with respect to so-called [philosophy of war](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/war/), is "this place is holy to my religion!" a valid justification *jus ad bellum*?

The answer for Christians is no, biblically speaking, and I don't even view this is as my personal opinion, but as a biblical fact. It might seem odd for me to be so aggressive in emphasizing this, but it is quite necessary, since plenty of religious conflicts right up until the present involve this matter, and lots of people seem to get this wrong.

<!-- slide-break -->

Now, we need to be extremely careful how we talk about things here. For example:

- God did tell the people of Israel to conquer [Canaan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canaan), and treat the covenantal [Promised Land](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Promised_Land) He gave to them as something special and important.
- Lots of stuff in the Law required going to *the* (definite article) temple in Jerusalem.
- During the Babylonian exile (for example), we know that the longing Jews had for their homeland was entirely proper and godly.
- Geographic Israel will play an important role in eschatology.
- Etc.

So the question is *not* "Does God ever want us to treat some specific place as special?" but "Does God *right now* (i.e., current dispensation) want us to treat some specific place as special?" Those are two different matters entirely.

Old Testament Jews living under the Law clearly were supposed to treat Jerusalem and the temple therein with great reverence. But we are not Old Testament Jews living under the Law. Moreover, that version of Jerusalem entirely ceased to exist after 70 A.D., when Titus essentially razed the city to the ground (including the temple).

<!-- slide-break -->

Unlike the Law---which involved many physical rituals---the new covenant of grace is mostly inward not outward. Moreover, Jesus's kingdom is not of this world (John 18:36); our homeland is not a place upon earth, but in heaven:

{{% scripture p="Hebrews 11:13-16 | ESV" %}}

**13** These [godly believers] all died in faith, not having received the things promised, but having seen them and greeted them from afar, and having acknowledged that they were strangers and exiles on the earth. **14** For people who speak thus make it clear that they are seeking a homeland. **15** If they had been thinking of that land from which they had gone out, they would have had opportunity to return. **16** But as it is, they desire a better country, that is, a heavenly one. Therefore God is not ashamed hto be called their God, for he has prepared for them a city.

{{% /scripture %}}

How ironic it is then that Christians throughout history fought wars over physical Jerusalem! They ought to have instead turned their eyes towards heavenly Jerusalem.

<!-- slide-break -->

What is even worse is that Christians have taken an obsession with the physical far further than just Jerusalem (which at least has going for it that it *used to be* a place of proper focus for believers, and will be again in the future). For example, plenty of Christians act like cathedrals and church buildings are holy. They are not. They are just buildings. What have buildings to do with our faith, or the teaching of the Word of God? Those are the important things, but they are not physical.

It is in fact blasphemous to think God specially dwells in things built by human hands; He only did that (insofar as one can call a symbolic physical column of cloud "doing that") with the tabernacle and then the temple. But now *we* are the temple (1 Peter 2:4-5), indwelt by the Holy Spirit. Focusing on physical buildings is therefore emphasizing exactly the wrong thing.

If the Vatican were to get wiped off the face of of the earth tomorrow, nothing changes. If the physical local church we attend were to get wiped off the face of the earth tomorrow, *nothing changes*. Why? Because the church is us, the assembly of human beings, not the building. After all, that is what the word "church" (Greek: [ἐκκλησία](https://biblehub.com/greek/1577.htm)) actually means.

<!-- slide-break -->

To take things even further, imagine for a moment that traditionally Christian regions (e.g., Western Europe, America) were to get conquered by theocratic Muslim regimes. What ought we make of that? Well, are we American first, or Christian first? Is our loyalty first to God, or to the physical land? You may scoff, but lots of nations throughout history have completely ceased to exist, and we see all too many examples of people who compromise themselves fighting for a country that is physical not spiritual. We are *not* citizens of this world, but are citizens of heaven.

<!-- slide-break -->

So when we read in history of Christians going on physical pilgrimages to supposedly holy places (and even going to war to support such pilgrimages), we should shake our heads, and have the same attitude as Paul did with the Galatians:

{{% scripture p="Galatians 4:8-11 | NIV11" %}}

**8** Formerly, when you did not know God, you were slaves to those who by nature are not gods. **9** But now that you know God—or rather are known by God—how is it that you are turning back to those weak and miserable forces? Do you wish to be enslaved by them all over again? **10** You are observing special days and months and seasons and years! **11** I fear for you, that somehow I have wasted my efforts on you.

{{% /scripture %}}

We are all of us on a spiritual pilgrimage towards Zion (cf. Psalm 84). What then are these physical journeys people are so proud of? Of what use are they? They are of no use at all.

Rather than caring about the physical, we should live out our daily lives wherever we are, with our eyes fixed firmly upon the Kingdom of Heaven. Whether we find ourselves in a beautiful stained-glass cathedral or a dirty slum, a Western democracy with freedom of religion or an oppressive regime wherein Christianity is heavily persecuted, all of this matters not. Instead, the only thing that matters is the spiritual dimension beyond this physical world.
